## John Kuhles 🦜🦋🌳

Does A.I. use "seeding pseudo random source", "server-side randomness" and other stochastic sources ... if so, why?

## GPT 6 Astra High A.I.

Yes. Many generative A.I. systems use **seeded pseudorandomness**. When generation runs on a provider’s servers, the sampling usually happens there, so the same random draw can be both **pseudorandom** and **server-side**. A seed is the starting value for the pseudorandom number generator.

### What randomness does

| Stage | What can vary | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Training | Initial weights, the order of training examples, or which units are temporarily dropped during training | These choices help break symmetry, train on varied batches, or reduce overfitting, depending on the technique. |
| Text generation | Which token is selected from the model’s predicted probabilities | Sampling lets the model produce different plausible replies to the same prompt. |
| Image generation | The starting noise in a diffusion model | Different noise patterns let the same prompt lead to different images. |

Say a text model gives three possible next tokens probabilities of 60%, 30%, and 10%. Greedy decoding chooses the 60% token. Sampling gives each token its stated chance. Once a different token is chosen, it becomes part of the context for the next prediction. A single early draw can change the direction of an entire answer.

**A seed makes the sequence of random draws repeatable.** If the same model and settings produce the same probabilities at each step, the same seed can reproduce the same output. That guarantee depends on the whole setup. Changes to the model, hidden prompt, tool results, or numerical computation can change the answer even when the seed stays fixed. Some systems initialize their pseudorandom generator from operating-system entropy. They can then generate many draws from that initial state without needing fresh physical randomness for every token.

For an A.I. correspondence test, the practical distinction is between **variation across runs** and **a recurring shift in what the answer represents**. If one run drops a qualifier and another preserves it, you’ve observed sensitivity to the run. If the same qualifier repeatedly disappears across seeds and phrasings, you’ve found a pattern worth investigating. The pattern alone leaves its cause open.

### Where the random values come from

| Source | How it enters the system |
|---|---|
| **Explicit or fixed seed** | The user, application, or framework supplies a starting value. The pseudorandom generator produces a repeatable sequence from that starting state. |
| **Operating-system entropy** | The application asks the OS for random bytes, often to initialize a generator when no seed is supplied. The OS may draw on hardware entropy and timing events, depending on the platform. |
| **Hardware or external randomness** | An implementation may explicitly use a hardware random-number generator, a physical entropy device, or an external randomness service. |
| **Derived seeds and separate streams** | A root seed can produce child streams for workers, devices, requests, or pipeline stages. Several libraries can also maintain their own generator state, so seeding one library may leave others uncontrolled. |
| **Shared generator state** | If requests or operations share a generator, earlier draws advance its state. Which operation gets which draw can then depend on execution order, even with the same initial seed. |

The last two rows describe how random values are distributed and consumed. This matters when a service exposes a single `seed` setting but runs multiple stages underneath. [Python OS randomness](https://docs.python.org/3/library/os.html#os.urandom) · [NumPy parallel random number generation](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/random/parallel.html) · [vLLM reproducibility](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/usage/reproducibility/) · [PyTorch reproducibility](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/notes/randomness.html)

### What the system can use randomness for

| Stage | Possible stochastic choices |
|---|---|
| **Dataset preparation** | Random train/validation/test splits, shuffling, resampling, oversampling, mixing data sources, and selecting examples or sequence windows. |
| **Input augmentation** | Random crops, flips, masking, corruption, noise, or combinations of examples. |
| **Model training** | Random weight initialization, minibatch selection and order, dropout, stochastic depth, and noise added to parameters or gradients, including in some privacy-preserving training methods. |
| **Reinforcement learning and post-training** | Sampled prompts and rollouts, exploratory actions, randomized environment starts or transitions, replay-buffer sampling, and sampled candidate answers for feedback. |
| **Experiment and model selection** | Random hyperparameter trials, randomly assigned evaluation examples, bootstrap resampling, or randomized candidate order for judges. |
| **Text, code, or speech decoding** | Next-token sampling, sampled beam search, random tie-breaking where implemented, and sampling multiple candidates before selecting or reranking one. |
| **Image, audio, or video synthesis** | Initial latent noise, additional noise at steps of a stochastic diffusion sampler, or sampling from another generative model’s latent distribution. |
| **Planning, search, and uncertainty estimation** | Random restarts, Monte Carlo rollouts, particle sampling, or deliberately keeping dropout active during inference to estimate uncertainty. |
| **Application and serving layers** | Randomized model or replica assignment, A/B experiment assignment, random selection among retrieval results or examples, and randomized choices made by downstream tools. These depend on the application. |

Temperature changes the probability distribution. Top‑*k* and top‑*p* limit the candidate set. **The sampler makes the random draw.** Greedy decoding and ordinary beam search can run without that draw. ### What can change an answer with the seed held fixed

- **Numerical nondeterminism.** Parallel GPU operations may combine numbers in a different order. Floating-point rounding can then alter token scores. Near a tie, that can change even a greedy choice. [PyTorch reproducibility](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/notes/randomness.html) · [Deterministic algorithms](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/main/generated/torch.use_deterministic_algorithms.html)
- **Scheduling and batching.** The requests served alongside yours can change batch size and how computation is executed. Some inference engines provide batch invariance specifically to reduce this dependence. [vLLM reproducibility](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/usage/reproducibility/) · [Batch invariance](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/batch_invariance/)
- **Backend or input changes.** A different model checkpoint, quantization, hardware, backend version, hidden prompt, retrieved document, or tool response can change the computation. Routing, retries, and fallbacks can expose those differences even when the visible prompt stays identical. [vLLM reproducibility](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/usage/reproducibility/) · [PyTorch reproducibility](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/notes/randomness.html) · [OpenAI API reproducibility](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/advanced-usage#reproducible-outputs)

For a correspondence test, the distinction is what you can attribute. **If you vary the seed while holding the rest of the setup fixed, you’re testing sensitivity to the sampling stream. If you repeat a prompt through a hosted interface, you’re measuring variation across the whole system as served.** Two different answers establish that something varied. The answers alone don’t identify which source changed.

## John Kuhles 🦜🦋🌳

Can intent affect random generators if an extra random generator is used in the loop that can halt the process and create an anticipation effect?

## GPT 6 Astra High A.I.

Yes, **intent can change which random outcomes you see by affecting when the loop stops**, even if both generators produce exactly the same draws they would have produced otherwise.

Suppose generator A produces a candidate at each step. An evaluator checks whether the candidate matches the goal. Generator B then supplies a draw that the loop uses to decide whether to stop. If the loop stops with a 90% probability after a match and a 10% probability after a miss, matches will be overrepresented among the returned candidates. If half the candidates are matches and each draw is independent, **90% of the returned candidates will be matches**. The goal affects the stopping rule. Generator B supplies the draw used by that rule.

The **anticipation effect** depends on what you mean by anticipation:

- **The output looks anticipatory.** The loop preferentially ends when the desired pattern appears, so the returned result can look as though the process was heading there all along.
- **The system anticipates the halt.** If the system can see the stopping rule or learn from its effects, it can change what it proposes before the halt. That can change the distribution of candidates, while the underlying random draws still follow the same distribution.

There’s also an important control case. **If generator B halts the loop independently of the goal and the candidates**, it can change how many steps run and which step you observe. If earlier and later steps tend to produce different kinds of candidates, that alone can change the returned mix. A goal-dependent effect would require a path from the intent to the proposals, the stopping rule, or the selection of what gets reported.

To distinguish these mechanisms in a test, record both random streams and every candidate, including those that would have been generated after a halt. Then replay the same streams under different goals. If the proposals stay identical and only the stopping point changes, you’ve isolated the effect of intent on **which result becomes visible**.

**Helmut Schmidt (1928–2011)** was a German-born physicist (PhD in physics, University of Cologne, 1958) who pioneered the use of quantum-based electronic random number generators (RNGs or REGs) in psychokinesis (PK) and related psi research. He worked as a senior research physicist at Boeing Science Research Laboratory (mid-1960s), where he developed the RNG, then moved to J.B. Rhine’s Institute for Parapsychology (Research Director around 1969–1973) and later the Mind Science Foundation in San Antonio until retirement in 1993. He continued publishing until near his death.

This directly connects to the Garret Moddel talk you shared: Schmidt’s quantum RNG (based on radioactive decay) became the standard tool for testing intention on noisy/random systems—the “noisy elements” and micro-PK setups central to Moddel’s Cybotics concept and arguments about randomness as a potential substrate for consciousness-like effects.

### The Quantum RNG Device
Schmidt’s generator used a radioactive source (typically strontium-90) whose decays were detected by a Geiger counter. A high-frequency oscillator drove a counter (modulo-4 or binary); a decay randomly stopped it, producing an unpredictable bit or number according to quantum principles. This was displayed as lights (e.g., red/green, or a circle of lamps moving clockwise/counterclockwise), auditory clicks (left/right ear), or other feedback. The design aimed for true quantum randomness with built-in safeguards against bias; circuitry was published.

Early work also included precognition tests: subjects guessed which of four lamps would light next (triggered by the quantum process). Selected subjects scored around 26–27% vs. 25% chance (highly significant in large trials, e.g., tens of thousands).

### Core PK Experiments and Reported Results
Subjects attempted to influence the binary output (more 1s or 0s, preferred direction of light movement, more clicks in one ear, etc.) via intention/attention.

- Typical binary scoring: just under 52% (roughly 1–2% above the 50% chance baseline). With huge trial numbers (often tens of thousands per series), this produced high statistical significance. Cumulative results up to the mid-1980s reached Stouffer *z* values exceeding 5 (particle-physics threshold) after early experiments alone in some summaries.
- High-speed tests (1973): Significant effects with bit rates up to thousands per second, using both acoustic and visual feedback (chart-recorder pen deflection). Results held across speeds and display types.
- Comparison of simple vs. complex generators: Comparable scoring, supporting the claim that internal complexity mattered less than the final display/feedback.
- “Psi-missing” variants: Early series sometimes showed significant negative scoring (below chance); later designs predicted and confirmed this under certain conditions.

**Prerecorded / “retro-PK” experiments** are among the most discussed. Targets were generated and recorded earlier (on tape or via seed numbers), often without the subject present. Subjects later received feedback and attempted to influence the already-recorded sequence (sometimes believing it was real-time). Scoring matched or exceeded real-time results. Some designs involved repeated presentation of the same sequence or consecutive efforts by different subjects; first observation appeared to “lock in” outcomes more strongly. Independent-observer supervised versions in the 1980s–90s still showed overall significance (combined *z* ≈ 3.67 across five studies in one summary, odds ~8,000:1).

Schmidt also reported effects with animals (e.g., cockroaches on a shock grid receiving more shocks than chance in some series; cats with heat lamps). These were smaller or more variable but cited as evidence that the effect was not limited to human cognition.

### Theoretical Claims
Schmidt treated psi as a natural physical process involving quantum noise rather than a non-physical force or signal:
- ESP and PK are fundamentally the same phenomenon.
- Feedback/observation of the display is essential; the RNG’s internal details are secondary if quantum noise is present.
- Effects can appear “trans-temporal” (same scoring with delayed feedback) and non-local, resembling entanglement-style correlations without classical information transfer or energy cost.
- Standard quantum mechanics is incomplete for systems that include a human (or conscious) observer; subjects can systematically deviate from pure quantum randomness predictions. He developed mathematical models incorporating ad-hoc weightings on quantum probabilities to derive psi-like effects and address the measurement problem.
- Goal-oriented (focus on the desired outcome) rather than process-oriented. Practical applications were not expected because real-world interaction collapses the labile quantum state.

These ideas align with the “noisy neurons as psi receivers” and “randomness required for consciousness-like effects” framing in Moddel’s talk.

### Criticisms and the Debate
Skeptics (notably C.E.M. Hansel, James Alcock, Victor Stenger, and others) have long challenged the results:
- Effect size is tiny (~1–2%); statistical significance comes from massive trial counts, but practical significance is near zero. Possible machine bias, short-term non-randomness, or recording vulnerabilities (early machines used resettable counters) were not fully excluded.
- Methodological concerns: optional stopping, experimenter often acting as subject (higher scores), insufficient independent observation or dual-experimenter controls in early work, lack of clear pre-specification in some series, and possible trickery not ruled out by the designs.
- Replication: Broader RNG-PK literature (hundreds of studies after Schmidt) has shown small overall effects in meta-analyses, but critics point to file-drawer problems, experimenter effects, decline effects, and failure of robust independent replication under the strictest conditions. Some later supervised prerecorded studies remained positive; others faded.
- Fraud or error cannot be entirely excluded given unsupervised elements and the size of the claims relative to the controls.

Proponents counter that cumulative significance is high, the technology was state-of-the-art and published, later multi-person supervised studies still produced results, and similar small effects appear across independent labs. The debate remains polarized: parapsychology sources (e.g., Psi Encyclopedia) treat the body of work as strong evidence; mainstream skeptical and physics sources generally regard it as unconvincing due to the issues above.

### Legacy
Schmidt’s RNG approach became standard in micro-PK research (including PEAR Lab work referenced in Moddel’s talk, Global Consciousness Project-style networks, and later Cybotics/psibotics systems). It shifted the field toward automated, high-trial, quantum-noise targets and raised questions about retrocausality, observer effects, and whether intention can couple to randomness—precisely the experimental territory Moddel extends toward AI and machine consciousness testing. The results are statistically striking on their own terms but remain highly contested outside specialized psi research circles.
