A few weeks ago, a client of mine—a senior executive—asked me a question that I haven’t been able to stop thinking about. People earlier in their careers at his firm are handing him AI work that they’ve never even read. And he asked me: if AI is doing the work, how does anyone ever learn what good looks like?

If you’re new here, I’m Dylan. I run an AI consultancy, so I hear a lot of questions regarding AI. But that same month, a law client asked me the same thing. Then a construction client, then an investment firm. People who have never met, all asking the same question in the same few weeks. So I wanted to dig a bit deeper and do some research. And it got stranger the deeper that I went, because this turns out to be way bigger than one group of workers. It touches basically everyone using AI, no matter how long you’ve been doing your job—experienced or not. So now let me show you what I found.

In researching this question of how we actually teach new people—how do they know what good looks like if they’ve not actually done the work themselves—we first have to understand the historical deal that we made with the jobs that we took. The deal that we made in those jobs is that when you take the job, the company and you usually subconsciously agree that you’re going to do many of the mundane tasks. But in doing those, you’ll learn how to do the job more effectively, which then allows you to climb the career ladder—to move from doing the mundane, boring work to doing more strategic work. And that boring work was important because it was like your tuition.

When you would do the tasks such as digging through documents, you’d figure out what matters and what doesn’t. Or if you’re building a first draft for a PowerPoint or a report or some sort of data analysis, you’d figure out what good looks like because you would get feedback from the people you’re reporting to. The same thing applies to heavy data tasks with numbers. When you would look at the numbers, you quickly figure out, through pattern recognition, where errors commonly popped up based on the tasks you were doing. So these are all different manifestations of doing the work and then learning how to do the task more effectively and judge what good looks like for the specific role over time.

But what has happened is, as AI has gotten better, we’ve started outsourcing many of these mundane, repetitive tasks to AI, so that that task—that tuition of learning through work—has slowly disappeared or is degrading. This isn’t simply a prediction by me. This is happening today. And I gathered a series of statistics just to show you what’s happening.

This first stat is showing that in the last three years, entry-level jobs have actually declined by 35% for many of the apprenticeship-type roles in law firms, consulting firms, banks, etc. Another interesting stat I came across is that the biggest banks—the biggest ones out there—for the new analyst classes that they hire in (probably once or twice a year), they’ve cut those by two-thirds. In a more macro analysis by Harvard, they studied around 62 million workers and they noticed that companies that were adopting AI, 18 months after they’ve adopted AI, roughly 10% of the junior roles were cut, but the senior roles were barely even touched.

And what I’ve noticed is when you have a new role and you’re adopting AI, you want to make sure that those hiring managers look at the role itself, the tasks associated with it, and they ask themselves: can AI do this partially or completely before I actually hire the person? So when they first ask themselves, can we hire AI before we hire a human? My guess is that’s what’s partly feeding into this negative 10%.

And to make this a bit more vivid, we have our career ladder here, right? And the bottom rungs of that ladder are being sawed off or broken off with AI because AI is filling those gaps, automating those tasks. So one’s ability to learn a given role through the process of doing the work is disappearing. But, to my point earlier, it’s not just junior roles, because the judgment and the ability to review AI’s output for an expert that’s been in the role for a while is different now. You’re not reviewing human work; you’re reviewing AI work. So the skill sets here as an expert need to change as well, which opens an opportunity for juniors because we need to train them on their ability to judge and decide if the output from an AI is good as well.

Because to that point, the output from a human—the mistakes they make—often look way different than an AI’s, irrespective of the task at hand. And as somebody that’s been in a role for a while, and you’ve been reviewing outputs from juniors in your company, you start to realize that the human output is obviously wrong in different ways because you’ve built an ability to identify those patterns. They tend to be sloppy in different ways; there’s obvious gaps, and things are rushed. So you can identify those patterns quickly. But with AI, it’s completely different. AI’s output tends to be polished, confident, and the errors are usually harder to find. So this turns into a completely different skill set, and you have to build your ability to identify those patterns. So both the juniors and the seniors in your company are starting from the same place.

Now, one quick note on where value is shifting. So, based on the profession you’re in, AI is being adopted at different speeds. Right now, for most professions, the value still somewhat resides in you doing the work. So by you doing the work, that is valuable. But for a small number of professions, that’s starting to change, where AI is replacing that gap in value, and the AI then does the work for you. So then you need to shift your attention to a higher-leverage area. And that for most professions is actually you checking the work from the AI and making sure it meets your expectations before you ship it out. But even that section is starting to be degraded by AI, and that’s specifically in the world of coding. What’s happening is the AI is actually checking its own work or having another AI check that work. And where the human focus is really on taste and deciding where to point the AI in the first place. So we’re moving up the value chain to higher and higher levels of strategic thinking.

But before we get ahead of ourselves, we should look at what other companies are doing right now and how they’re adapting to the situation. I’m saying, “I’m hiring new people. How do I actually train them on how to judge good output if they’re not doing the work themselves?” Well, there are two things that I’ve noticed.

One is Deloitte. Deloitte’s published a bunch of information on this, showing how they’re training up their new associates because they have huge pools of associates they hire. And usually in the past, like we’ve talked about, they would simply have them do the work, get feedback from their leaders, and then from that they would learn. But now what they’re doing is these new programs—they’ve completely restructured them. Where instead of you doing the work, AI often does most of the work for you. So instead, what they’re doing is they’re training these new associates on how to identify mistakes the AI has made and, more importantly, explaining exactly why that’s wrong. And this is the key here: when you want to train somebody on identifying what good looks like, they need to understand the underlying why, and this is where that expertise comes into play. So you have your senior and your junior. Your junior is going to learn how to judge output with the feedback from the senior—them telling them exactly why something is wrong and being able to articulate it effectively. And that’s key because a lot of experts, they delegated the learning to the work itself. But me as an expert now, I have to be able to articulate myself effectively, saying exactly why something is good and why something is bad. So we need to take what’s in your head that’s intuitive and make it explicit for the others to learn effectively. This can be a daunting task, but it is much needed, and it’s way worth the effort if you can train your associates on this going forward. Not just training them, but eventually training AI too. So we then can kind of move up those ladders of value like I mentioned previously.

And that’s the first tactic: having the associates judge the output from the AI, getting better at figuring out what good looks like through that process. So they’re skipping doing the work and going straight to the judging.

Another thing I came across is a lot of folks on Wall Street—so big banks, hedge funds, etc. A tactic they’re taking here for their new hires is they’re doing drills. And they’re doing this on targeted skills. So they’re not doing this on all skills because this is a manual thing that takes a lot of time. You don’t want to waste your time because that’s somewhat counterintuitive to putting AI in the first place. We’re leveraging AI to be more efficient so we can get more out of our employees, not making them intentionally pause and slow down. But that’s actually what we’re going to do here. So in this, they’ve chosen specific skills that matter a lot and they want to pause, slow down, and practice those skills with the associates. So what they’ll do is they’ll have the associate do the task by hand manually. After they’ve done it—it could be a rough draft; it doesn’t have to be polished or anything like that—they just need to do the task effectively. After they’ve done the task, they then have the AI do the same exact task. They have both do the tasks and then they compare the outputs. Say, “Okay, here’s a human output. Here’s the AI output.” And through this process, we’re identifying both the weaknesses in the associate as well as the weaknesses in the AI. That’s beneficial in two ways. One is we’re training the human on how to do this better, but more importantly, we can take those learnings from what we did mistakes on and what the AI did mistakes on, and we can improve the AI on this task going forward so it can get better and better and better.

And the analogy that came to mind for me is practice. So in any sport, you practice before you actually compete. That’s exactly what’s happening here. There are certain skills that we want to sustain, at least for now, and to do that we need to intentionally set aside time to practice that skill. But on that point, there’s one idea here that we need to really hit home and understand: is that there are certain skills that we need to intentionally practice, but we also need to be willing to let go of those skills over time. Some common examples: being able to navigate the world without a GPS is extremely hard for most people today, and we’ve all collectively kind of accepted that it’s okay that we can outsource that skill set to GPS from now on. The same thing applies to calculators. Being able to do manual math in your head or on paper is a skill that’s being degraded by many generations, and we’re accepting that because we’re okay with computers and calculators doing that math for us. This is just a small sample of things that we’ve accepted and outsourced to technology. The same thing is going to apply to AI. But if we want to practice, we can’t just assume the work’s going to do it for us. We need to set aside time and really try hard to do it ourselves.

Now, as I mentioned previously, the primary way you’re going to do this today, instead of doing the work, is working on your ability to judge the output. There are two ways we can do this. Either we do it manually, where you compare your output against the AI’s output, and you see the weaknesses you had and they had, and through that process, you can coach the AI up and improve its ability to do this task over time. That’s the first piece. The next one—and the one I’d recommend doing more than you actually doing the work manually—is for the majority of skills and outputs from AI, you just train your employees on their ability to judge the AI’s output. So there’s a lot of knowledge in here that you need to get out into your juniors’ heads, so they can then start judging the AI’s output. The only way you can do that is through practice with them in that process.

And one other thing that I’ve noticed with a lot of these companies that I researched and looked at is that I could see that the skills that they’re trading are changing over time. So we have really two lists: skills we’re willing to trade and skills that we want to keep because they’re important for whatever reason. Over time, that line will shift. Many of the skills that we’re unwilling to trade today, we will start trading tomorrow because AI gets better. So in the spectrum, we have things that the AI handles on the left and things that we handle on the right. But execution, as we mentioned, tends to be very affected by AI. It can do a lot of the execution for us. It also starts to do the drafting for us depending on the scenario. It can draft PowerPoints and reports and emails and different types of Excel sheets—all types of things for us if we train it up effectively and we can review the output effectively. As I mentioned previously, development and coding—they’re starting to outsource the review there as well because the AI generates the code and they don’t necessarily have the bandwidth to review all that code, so they have to outsource the review to the AI as well. So the name of the game is for us to determine how good AI is today, if it’s good enough to actually trade that skill off to AI or not. And if it’s better, more reliable, more consistent, and high quality, we then can trade that skill off from a human to an AI and then that human then diverts their attention towards something that’s higher leverage and something that AI still struggles with. It’s not that that role disappears; they’re just shifting their attention to something higher leverage and more strategic.

So I want you to walk away with something very practical from this video, and that’s exactly what the slide’s highlighting. So today, what I want you to do is I want you to sit down and determine, based off of the tasks that you have your juniors doing currently, how do you decide what skills to keep and what skills you’re okay with degrading and outsourcing to AI? And the ability to judge the AI’s output is one that you want to keep currently. So once you’ve decided on those lists, you then need to intentionally start practicing the skills you want to keep. And there are two ways we can do this. One is the judgment one which I mentioned you probably want to do for most of your skills. The other one is intentionally manually practicing that skill and then comparing the output against AI. And that’s the minority, because that’s when you’re intentionally slowing yourself down, so you have to be careful on how many skills we apply to that specific tactic. And after we’ve decided on what we’re going to practice and keep—both judgment and manual workflows—we then start trading off the skills we don’t necessarily need anymore. And where AI has proven that it’s more reliable than a human and the quality meets our standards, that’s when you can start outsourcing that to AI. And then finally that list that we have of things that we keep and things that we outsource to AI—we’re going to have to revisit the list of the things that we keep probably every couple of months as new AI models come out. Because the AI gets more intelligent, it can start eating away further and further at their tasks that we then can shift our attention towards other things that are more strategic and higher leverage. And that’s it.
