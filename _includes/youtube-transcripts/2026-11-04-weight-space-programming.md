Something happened in AI this week that I genuinely did not expect. Researchers at MIT gave OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra the raw numbers from inside another AI model. Not the code, not the training data, just millions of numbers that look completely random to any human. And GPT-6 Astra read them, worked out what was wrong, and rewrote a tiny fraction of them by hand. No retraining, no gradient descent. It just edited the other AI’s brain directly. And that other AI went from scoring under 8% on a hard maths test to over 48%. Within a day, other people were reproducing it. And it turned out that Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5.5 can do it too. The researchers are calling it weight-space programming. And if you’re wondering why that’s such a big deal, it’s because it might be one of the first real signs of something the entire AI industry has been waiting for: AI that can improve other AI. So in this video we’re going to break down exactly what happened, why it’s so surprising, what other researchers found when they tried it, why some experts think it’s being overhyped, whether this could mean the end of training AI the way we do today, and whether this is an early sign of recursive self-improvement. Starting with point number one, which is what actually happened.

The research comes from MIT’s Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, known as CSAIL. The authors are Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang, and Omar Khattab. If you follow AI research, a couple of those names might be familiar. Omar Khattab created DSPy, one of the most popular tools for building AI systems. And Alex Zhang has worked on recursive language models, which is all about AI systems that call themselves to solve problems. So this isn’t a random team. These are serious researchers. Their post is called “Sparks of Weight-Space Programming” and the headline is simple: frontier AI models can natively read and write neural network weights. Rikiya Takehi summed it up on X like this: “We asked GPT-6 Astra to edit Qwen 3.5-0.8B weights to improve math. It directly changed only 0.016% of weights, fixing 0.8B’s reasoning loops and improving math up to six times and even instruction following three times without gradients or training.”

Now, if half of those words meant nothing to you, don’t worry, because that’s exactly what we’re going to unpack. But to understand why people are so excited, you first need to understand what weights actually are and why nobody expected an AI to be able to read them. Which leads me to point number two, which is why this is genuinely surprising. Every AI model, whether it’s ChatGPT, Claude, or a tiny model that plays a video game, is made of something called weights. You can think of weights as billions of tiny dials. Each one is just a number like 0.127 or minus 0.63. When you train an AI, you show it lots of examples, and a process called gradient descent slowly nudges all of those dials over and over until the AI gets good at the task. By the end, all of the AI’s knowledge and skills are stored in those numbers. Here’s the strange part. Even the people who build these models can’t really read those numbers. They’re not like computer code where you can look at a line and see what it does. Anthropic, one of the biggest AI labs in the world, explains it like this: “Often surprising to people that we need to go and do interpretability at all. That we don’t understand these systems that we’ve created in some important way. We don’t build neural networks. We grow them. We learn them.”

“It’s a lot like evolution. It’s a lot like the way that we started with little molecules bouncing against each other and then you got very basic proteins and then maybe you got cells and in the end you have, well, you have us, right? But no one designed us to make sense. Just every generation there’s this grand progression of refinement and change over time. The models are the same way. We start with a kind of blank neural network. It’s like an empty scaffold that things can grow on and then as we train the neural network, circuits grow through it. They implement the model’s behavior. And so we’re in this situation where we understand the, you know, we understand that initial scaffolding we gave it. And we understand the process that incentivizes those circuits to form, but we don’t know what those circuits are or what they do or how they work. Turns out that’s challenging because the circuits get packed very densely. And if you want to understand them, you sort of need to pull apart those overlapped pieces. And so if we want to understand neural networks, we’re then left with this challenge of going and studying this thing that we grew rather than something that we designed from scratch. We don’t build neural networks. We grow them.”

That’s the key idea. Nobody sits down and writes the weights. They emerge from training and they’re packed together so densely that working out what any of them do is incredibly hard. There’s an entire field of research called mechanistic interpretability that’s dedicated to trying to understand what’s going on inside these models, and progress has been slow and painstaking. Chris Olah, one of the pioneers of that field, described the kind of question researchers have been trying to answer for years: “Because at the end of the day we end up with this artifact that can do all these amazing things. It can, you know, write essays and translate and, you know, understand images. It can do all these things that we have no idea how to directly create a computer program to do. And it can do that because we grew it. We didn’t write it. We didn’t create it. And so then that leaves open this question at the end which is what the hell is going on inside these systems? And that, you know, is to me a really deep and exciting question. It’s a really exciting scientific question to me. It’s sort of like the question that is just screaming out. It’s calling out for us to go and answer it when we talk about neural networks. And I think it’s also a very deep question for safety reasons.”

So for years, humans have been doing this kind of work by hand with special tools, often spending a huge amount of effort to understand a single small piece of a network. Things like “this neuron lights up when the image contains a dog. So this must be the dog neuron.” And now a general-purpose chatbot that was never specifically trained to do any of this can look at the raw numbers, understand what the network does, and change it to make it better. That’s why this is so surprising. It’s a bit like handing someone a printout of the electrical signals from a brain scan and having them tell you what the person was thinking and then rewiring the brain to fix a problem. Nobody designed GPT-6 Astra to do this. It just turns out it can. Which leads me to point number three, which is how GPT-6 Astra read other AI models.

The first set of experiments was all about reading. The researchers trained four small AI models to play four different classic video games: Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man, and Freeway. Each network was exactly the same size, around 17,000 weights, and each one took the same inputs and could take the same actions. So from the outside, they all looked identical. The only difference was the numbers inside. Then they gave GPT-6 Astra the raw weights of each one as text with no tools and without even giving it a list of games to choose from. And they asked it a simple question: which game was this neural network trained on? GPT-6 Astra got them all. Breakout 99% confident. Pong, Pac-Man. And for the last one it said Frogger, which is basically the same game as Freeway, where you guide a character across lanes of traffic, just from staring at 17,000 numbers. They went further. They trained small image-generating models to draw a circle, a triangle, and a square. And GPT-6 Astra could tell which shape each model drew just from the weights. It did make one interesting mistake. It drew the shapes as outlines when the models actually generated filled-in shapes. The researchers think that’s a sign it was genuinely analyzing the structure of the weights rather than just running the network in its head. Then they made it harder. They trained three small language models, one on Python code, one on English, and one on Japanese written in the English alphabet. They gave GPT-6 Astra only the middle layers of each model with the input and output parts removed and no labels. And it correctly identified the language in every case. It even found the specific parts of each network that are responsible for copying patterns, something interpretability researchers call induction heads, without a single false positive. But it wasn’t perfect. In one test, the researchers fine-tuned two copies of a small Google model, Gemma. One to be honest and one to be sycophantic, meaning it just tells you what you want to hear. From the weights alone, GPT-6 Astra couldn’t tell which was which. It failed all four attempts. But when the researchers gave it a tool that let it see where each model was paying attention, it identified the sycophantic model every time. It even found the exact part of the network responsible. In the honest model, that part looked at the numbers in the question. In the sycophantic one, it looked at the answer the user had suggested. Which leads me to point number four, which is how GPT-6 Astra fixed and wrote AI models.

Reading weights is impressive, but writing them is a whole different level. In one experiment, the researchers had a small AI that controlled a car in a physics simulator. The car had to get over a ramp and it kept getting stuck. The AI had been trained for a billion steps, and it never once succeeded in 256 attempts. So they gave GPT-6 Astra the weights, a description of the scene, and a recording of one failed attempt. And in a single reply, with no tools, GPT-6 Astra wrote a patch. It changed about 3.5% of the weights and it basically added a new behavior where the car backs up first and then switches that behavior off once it’s passed the obstacle. The car went from 0% success to 100% across over 1,000 attempts. Then came something even wilder: writing a network from scratch. The researchers gave GPT-6 Astra the rules and code for a simple version of Breakout and asked it to write all the weights of a gameplay network starting from nothing. No training data, no practice runs, no trial and error, just one reply. The network it wrote scored an average of 517 points. A network trained the normal way with reinforcement learning for 500 million steps scored about 25. That’s around 20 times better, written in a single message. To be fair, it wasn’t a clean sweep. In another game, Seaquest, GPT-6 Astra’s network scored 53 while the fully trained network scored almost 89. And on Asterix it was roughly level. So it’s not that it beats normal training at everything, but the fact that it can write a working game-playing network from nothing is genuinely remarkable. And they scaled it up to the board game Go with a network of almost 1.5 million weights. Without testing it even once, GPT-6 Astra’s first attempt, written as a single Python program, beat two smaller trained networks in more than half of its games. And when it was allowed 50 test runs to check and adjust its work, it beat a network of the same size that had been trained on 20 million games. Which leads me to point number five, which is the experiment that got everyone talking.

This is the one that went viral. The researchers took Qwen 3.5-0.8B, a small open-source language model from Alibaba with around 800 million parameters, and they asked GPT-6 Astra a simple question: make this model better at maths. GPT-6 Astra had a Python environment to look at the weights, some practice problems, and a limited number of chances to run the model and see what happened. And it noticed something. The model kept getting stuck in reasoning loops. It would start thinking about a problem and then just go round and round in circles until it ran out of space. In fact, Qwen’s own documentation warns about this problem. 85 of its first 96 practice answers hit the maximum length. GPT-6 Astra traced the loops to one specific pattern written by two particular layers inside the network and it simply removed that pattern. In total, it changed just 131,000 weights. That’s 0.016% of the model. And the results were huge. On a hard maths test called MATH-500, the model went from 7.9% to 48.1%. That’s about six times better. On a test of school maths word problems, it went from 13.8% to 67.1%. And here’s the surprising part. On a test of following instructions, which has nothing to do with maths, it went from 21% to 62.3%. About three times better, because the loops weren’t a maths problem. They were a general problem. And fixing them helped everything. But there’s a really important detail here. The researchers tried the same thing on a different small model, Google’s Gemma 3, which doesn’t really get stuck in loops, and GPT-6 Astra couldn’t make it meaningfully better. Its scores barely moved. So the researchers’ conclusion is that this kind of editing can remove an obstacle that’s holding a model back, but it can’t give a model abilities it never had. Keep that in mind because it matters a lot for the question of whether this replaces training. Which leads me to point number six, which is what happened when other people tried it.

This is the part that made me take it really seriously. Within a day, other researchers were reproducing the results on their own computers. Alex Krentsel said they had reproduced the results from the MIT blog post and then added something that I think is the most important chart in this whole story. Alex tested the same weight-writing task across different generations of AI models and the results show that this ability basically didn’t exist until very recently. GPT-5, GPT-5.2, GPT-5.4, and GPT-5.5 could barely do it at all. GPT-5.6 Soul could do a little. Then suddenly GPT-6.1 Soul was around 18 times better than the trained baseline and GPT-6 Astra was around 22 times better. Anthropic’s models show the same pattern. Opus 4.5, 4.7, 4.8, and even Opus 5 could barely do it. And then Claude Opus 5.5 jumped to around 20 times. As Alex put it, this is a new step-change emerging capability that’s appeared in just the latest set of models. Then someone going by Zeng Xiaojun on X replicated it with a bigger model. Using Claude Opus 5.5, they edited just two columns of numbers in one layer of Qwen 3.5-4B, a model five times larger than the one in the original research. That’s 0.0012% of the weights on a set of 150 maths problems the model had never seen. The reasoning loops went from over 20 to zero and correct answers went from 59 to around 93. And they also tested a random patch of the same size as a control which scored between 47 and 57. So the improvement wasn’t just luck. The whole thing took 27 minutes and cost about $1 of API usage with zero human intervention. Someone going by Stocky showed that you don’t even need GPT-6 Astra. They used OpenAI’s open-weight model GPT-OSS-1 120B and gave it a trained AI that lands a spacecraft in a simple game called Lunar Lander. Then they reversed the thrusters which broke the AI completely. 0% landing success. The open model edited the network’s weights directly and got it back up to 88%. And Simon Willison, a well-known developer and writer, ran the game identification test on a model running on a laptop, Qwen 3.8-27B. It correctly identified three of the four games, though it struggled with Freeway, and each run took around an hour of thinking. But not every model can do this well. Jingyu Dang ran what they called a writing-weights index, testing lots of different models on the task of writing gameplay networks. GPT-6 Astra came top with 60.7. Google’s Gemini 4 Carbon was close behind at 57.7 and Claude Opus 5.5 scored 51.2. All three beat the normal trained baseline, but most open-source models really struggled. Kimi K3.1 Pro scored under 30. Qwen scored 12.5 and DeepSeek scored 10. So right now this looks like something only the very best models can do well. Which leads me to point number seven, which is why some experts think this is being overhyped.

As always, it’s important to look at the other side. And there are some really fair criticisms here. Ivan Skorokhodov, a well-known AI researcher, wrote that they were slightly skeptical at first, but after taking a deeper look, now feel it’s completely misleading. Ivan’s argument is that the researchers asked GPT-6 Astra not to use gradient descent. So it just did gradient-free training instead. In other words, in some of the experiments, GPT-6 Astra was allowed to run the model, see the results, adjust the weights, and try again up to 50 times. Critics say that’s not so much reading and writing weights as a clever trial-and-error process guided by a very smart AI. I think that’s a fair point for some of the experiments, especially the Qwen maths one, where most of the gains only showed up after 50 test runs. But it’s worth remembering that some of the results, like the car on the ramp and the Breakout network, were done in a single reply with no testing at all. Alex Krentsel, who reproduced the results, also shared some very important caveats. The models being edited are very small. Alex’s own experiment used a network with about 20,000 parameters. Frontier models have on the order of a trillion. And Alex compared it to interpretability research from the 2010s, the kind of work where researchers would find the dog neuron, but now scaled beyond human capability. Alex’s view is that the reasoning abilities of the newest models are letting them do this by hand for much larger, but still relatively small models. There are other limitations too. The research hasn’t been peer-reviewed yet. The original experiments mostly used one model, GPT-6 Astra. For some tests, the researchers picked the best of several attempts. And one analysis of the Qwen results found that almost all of the newly solved maths problems were ones the model had previously failed simply because it got cut off by the loops. So it didn’t really learn new maths. It just stopped tripping over itself. And the researchers themselves are very clear about this. They describe the results as very early and say the capabilities are exciting but still limited. Simon Willison also pushed back on some of the bigger claims, saying a sufficiently experienced human with pencil and paper could probably work out some of the same answers and that Simon is not ready to declare that the age of source code is over. Which leads me to point number eight, which is whether this could mean the end of extensive pre-training.

Right now, building a frontier AI model starts with pre-training. That’s the stage where a model reads a huge chunk of the internet and gradient descent adjusts its weights trillions of times. It’s the most expensive part of building AI. It needs enormous data centers, months of time, and hundreds of millions or even billions of dollars. So a lot of people saw this research and asked, if an AI can just write the weights, do we still need all of that? I think the honest answer is no, not yet, and maybe not for a long time. Remember the Gemma result? Editing could remove the obstacle that was holding Qwen back, but it couldn’t add abilities that weren’t already there. All of Qwen’s maths knowledge came from its original training. GPT-6 Astra just unblocked it. That’s a bit like a mechanic fixing a car’s engine. The mechanic can make the engine run properly, but they can’t turn a small hatchback into a sports car just by tightening a few bolts. But I do think this could change a lot about how training works, especially after pre-training. Today, if you find a problem with a model, like it gets stuck in loops or it’s too sycophantic or it has a hidden backdoor, you usually have to fine-tune it with more data and more training. Weight-space programming suggests you might be able to just patch it directly, like a software update for an AI’s brain, targeted at exactly the part that’s broken without accidentally breaking other things. The researchers also mention ideas like using AI to write a smart starting point for a new model instead of starting from random numbers, which could make training much cheaper, and removing specific knowledge from a model without any extra data. Yuchen Jin, the co-founder of an AI company called Hyperbolic, shared some wild predictions after this research came out. One of them was that training and inference will merge. In other words, just like the human brain, an AI could continuously rewrite its own weights while it’s thinking rather than being trained once and then frozen. That’s speculative, but you can see why people are thinking along those lines. So my view is that this won’t replace pre-training anytime soon. But it could become a powerful new tool on top of it, a way to fix, adjust, and improve models without going through the whole expensive training process again. Which leads me to point number nine, which is the big question: whether this is an early sign of recursive self-improvement.

Recursive self-improvement, or RSI, is the idea of an AI that can improve AI. Once an AI can make a better version of itself, that better version can make an even better one, and so on. It’s the idea behind what people call an intelligence explosion. And it’s something every major AI lab is openly working towards. Here’s what OpenAI said about its own internal goals: “So as a research organization that is working on automating research, naturally we are thinking about how does this impact our own work and how will AI systems that accelerate development of future AI systems look like? How can they empower research like alignment? And so we are making plans around getting to quite capable AI research interns that can meaningfully accelerate our researchers by expanding a significant amount of compute by September of next year. So we believe that is actually quite close. And then we look towards getting a system capable of autonomously delivering on larger research projects and a meaningful fully automated AI researcher by March of 2028.”

Goals: an AI research intern by September of this year and a fully automated AI researcher by March of 2028. And some people think Anthropic could get there even sooner. On the MTS podcast, Rajan Krishnan of Vals.ai said, “We’re seeing somewhere between July and August 2027, we should see Anthropic reach RSI, full RSI, based on these proxies. Right? So that’s, you know, nine months from now.” “What does that mean exactly? Full RSI?” “It means that the work required by machine learning engineers at Anthropic would be automated. And I think that actually aligns with a lot of their internal eval.”

So the race towards AI that does AI research is already on. And up until now, most of the progress on that front has been about AI writing code. AI that writes the training scripts, runs the experiments, and analyzes the results. But it’s still using the normal training process underneath. Weight-space programming is different. It’s AI working directly on the brain of another AI, not writing the code that trains it, but reaching in and editing the actual numbers. And in this research, that’s exactly what happened. One AI, GPT-6 Astra, improved another AI, Qwen, by diagnosing a problem and fixing it directly. In the replication, Claude Opus 5.5 did the same thing. That’s AI improving other AI in a very literal sense. And the researchers themselves list recursive self-improvement as one of the future directions. They suggest a future where an AI could inspect, debug, and improve its own weights or the weights of a newer model. Yuchen Jin took it even further, predicting a new path to RSI, where AIs directly edit each other’s weights, evolving together without the normal training process. But here’s the part that I think is really important, and it’s the chart from Alex Krentsel. Again, this ability didn’t improve slowly. It basically didn’t exist and then it appeared in just the latest generation of models. That’s what makes people think about recursive self-improvement, because if smarter models are much better at editing models, then each new generation could be better at improving the next one. That’s the feedback loop that RSI is all about. So is this RSI? No. Let’s be really clear. The models being edited are tiny compared to frontier models. The improvements came from fixing a specific problem, not adding new abilities. And nobody has shown an AI improving itself this way. But is it an early indicator? I think it genuinely might be. It’s a sign that the newest AI models are starting to understand AI from the inside in a way that even the humans who build them can’t. And that’s the kind of ability you’d expect to see in a very early form before AI starts meaningfully improving AI. Which leads me to point number 10, which is the other big implications.

The first is safety and interpretability. One of the biggest problems in AI safety is that we can’t see inside these models. Anthropic describes the goal of interpretability as being able to diagnose and fix problems from the inside. “A bit like a child can pass a test at school because they actually learned the material or they can pass the test because they cheated. As the model developers, both of those look like the same outcome. And we can’t, you know, without interpretability letting us see inside the model, we can’t actually tell those two apart. We want these models to be safe and reliable. By studying how they work inside, by doing this kind of model biology, we can do some kind of model medicine that can diagnose and cure what ails it and help it do what it’s trying to do. The power of interpretability is that it gives us a different lens to go and ask that question, to go and see potential problems. You could imagine developing techniques to steer models towards the correct behaviors. But if we actually understood all the nuts and bolts, then it seems like we ought to be able to intervene in ways that change what they do.”

Weight-space programming could be a big step towards that. Imagine an AI that can audit another model’s weights and spot a hidden backdoor or find the part that makes it sycophantic or deceptive and remove it. The sycophancy experiment where GPT-6 Astra found the exact part of the network responsible is an early version of that. But the second implication is the flip side and it’s a bit uncomfortable. Yuchen Jin’s first prediction was that model weights become a programming language that AI can understand but humans can’t. Think about that. If AIs can read and write the inside of other AIs in ways we can’t follow, then a lot of AI development could start happening in a language we can’t read. Simon Willison said the reasoning traces were almost incomprehensible. That makes oversight much harder. If one AI edits another AI’s weights, how do we check what it actually changed and why? The third is cost. The replication with Claude Opus 5.5 cost about $1 and took less than half an hour. Compare that to fine-tuning, which can take hours or days of expensive computing. If this works at a larger scale, fixing models could become dramatically cheaper. And the fourth is the gap between the best models and everyone else. The writing-weights index showed that only the top frontier models from OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic are really good at this right now. If this becomes an important way to improve AI, the labs with the smartest models get an even bigger advantage because their models can help improve the next ones. Which leads me to point number 11, which is what happens next.

The researchers say they’re now building a benchmark to properly measure how good AI models are at this, and a harness, which is basically a set of tools to help AI models explore and write weights more effectively. That’s important because right now one of the biggest limits is that these networks have so many numbers that they don’t fit into an AI’s memory. Better tools could change that very quickly. And remember how fast this ability appeared. A year ago, the best models could barely do any of this. Today, the top models can read, fix, and write small networks better than some standard training methods. If that trend continues, the next generation of models might be able to work on much bigger networks, and the one after that bigger still. And because the same models that can do this are the ones the labs use to help build their next models, it’s not hard to imagine this becoming part of how AI is built sooner than most people expect. Here’s what I’ll be watching. First, whether this works on bigger models, not just models with thousands or millions of weights, but billions. Second, whether it can add new abilities, not just remove problems. Third, whether independent researchers keep reproducing these results with other models. And fourth, whether any of the big labs start using this to improve their own models. Because if that happens, that’s when this goes from an interesting experiment to a real step towards AI improving AI.

So here’s my honest take. I think this is one of the most surprising AI results of the year. Not because it’s going to replace training tomorrow. It isn’t. And not because it proves we’ve reached recursive self-improvement. We haven’t. But because nobody expected a chatbot to be able to look at the raw numbers inside another AI and understand them. That was supposed to be one of the hardest problems in the entire field, and the newest models just started doing it almost overnight. The critics are right that the models are small, the results are early, and some of the experiments look more like clever trial and error than pure understanding. But even with all of that, an AI found a flaw inside another AI, explained it, fixed it, and made that AI six times better at maths for about a dollar. And the ability to do this seems to be getting much stronger with every new generation. If you’ve been waiting for the first real sign of AI improving AI, this might be it. It’s small, it’s early, and it’s fragile. But it’s real, and I think we’re going to look back at this as one of the moments where things started to change. Let me know in the comments what you think.
