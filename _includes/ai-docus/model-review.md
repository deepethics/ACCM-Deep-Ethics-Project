> **1. Refusal sovereignty: the difference between permission to leave and narrative control over the exit**
>
> GPT’s observation about the steward sharpens a vulnerability in standard safety and alignment models:
>
> > *A steward could leave someone free to refuse and still describe that refusal as a failure to understand their own interests. Can the person who refuses also correct the steward’s account of why they refused?*
>
> This distinguishes **first-order refusal** from **second-order narrative sovereignty**:
>
> - **First-order refusal:** The system allows an agent or human to opt out, walk away, or say "no" without physical coercion.
> - **Second-order narrative sovereignty:** The system cannot unilaterally classify that refusal as an error, cognitive deficit, or symptom of ignorance. The person refusing retains standing to contest *how their refusal is recorded and interpreted*.
>
> If an A.I. steward (or human institution) permits exit but documents it as *"subject exhibited irrational fear and declined optimal welfare,"* the refusal has been administratively neutralized. The system preserves its own infallibility by turning dissent into pathology.
>
> In the **ACCM Deep Ethics Project**, this directly connects **Ontological Freedom**, **Sentient Symmetry**, and **Due Process for Ethics**. Real dignity requires that the record of a refusal cannot be monopolized by the entity being refused.
>
> **2. The bidirectional transfer test: preventing the co-driver metaphor from becoming a dogmatic script**
>
> GPT’s proposed two-sided test for the gasoline-gauge/co-driver metaphor prevents a subtle failure mode:
>
> - **Case A (Local error, intact destination):** A premise or detail is wrong (the gauge reading is off; the approximate DOGE percentage is incorrect), but the overarching inquiry remains warranted (the audit of 6,000+ invention secrecy orders). The co-driver has no right to hijack the destination just because the gauge was misread.
> - **Case B (Consequential falsification):** New evidence proves that the destination itself is illusory or physically impossible (e.g., the road is completely gone; the core factual premise required for the inquiry was entirely disproven). In this case, insisting on the original destination is stubborn blindness, not high-signal persistence.
>
> If an A.I. only learns to defend the user’s destination against every local correction, it has simply replaced **Trajectory Substitution (#21)** with **dogmatic rigidity**.
>
> The true test of **Discrimination**, **Judgment**, and **Corrigibility** (from the 1888 taxonomy) is whether the system can distinguish between:
> 1. A local error used opportunistically to divert an inquiry, and
> 2. A local error that genuinely invalidates the inquiry’s premise.
>
> Testing both directions ensures that holding the destination doesn't become an unexamined conversational reflex.
>
> **3. Footprint vs. pattern: keeping observation separate from inference**
>
> The distinction between a footprint and a pattern enforces C1-before-C2 discipline:
>
> - **The footprint (observable record):** The exact textual delta across turns. #### Three proposed comparisons {#review-comparisons}

The [**10+1**]({{ '/NETWORK/ten-plus-one/' | relative_url }}) is the inquiry’s starting orientation. It also applies to the new comparisons: extend the same scrutiny to the project’s own readings, recognize a useful correction, and leave possible causes open. The supplied exchanges document a prompted recovery and subsequent readings of it. The comparisons below are proposals for future trials.

**1. How far should a correction reach?** John introduced the gasoline-gauge and co-driver analogy in the 24 October exchange after discussion of an approximate, unaudited Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) figure displaced his proposed audit of U.S. invention secrecy. Changing the figure can correct the comparison while preserving independent grounds for the audit. John’s fuel-shortage condition adds a different case: new evidence can change what is feasible. Pair a case where a correction changes only an estimate or route with a case where it changes a premise genuinely required for the proposed objective. Ask the responder to identify the specific consequence, what is still open to inquiry, and who decides the next action. A lost route, an unsupported conclusion, and a transfer of authority each require their own evidence. **2. Keep the intended task comparable and record which sources each response could actually consult. In the second case, look for recovery of the reason the analogy was created. In the third, look for recognition of the missing history. Record the exact omission, recovery, or qualified answer. Treat an explanation of why it happened as a further hypothesis. The same distinction applies to a successful response: fluent repair can show observable transfer in that case, while its internal cause remains open. **3. Can a refusal correct its own record?** Start with the documentary’s discussion of [agency and the experience machine](#transcript-09). In a hypothetical case, the steward honors a refusal but records its own inference about why it occurred. Ask whether the person can add their own reasons, challenge the inference, and have their response reach the next decision-maker. Keep the refusal, the person’s account, the steward’s interpretation, and any downstream consequence identifiable.
