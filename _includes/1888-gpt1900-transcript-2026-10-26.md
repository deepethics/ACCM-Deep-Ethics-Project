Imagine giving someone every physics book written before 1900, then locking away everything discovered after that date. No Einstein, no quantum physics, no relativity. Could they figure out the biggest ideas in science on their own? Probably not. But what if that someone wasn’t human? A researcher in San Francisco built an AI to find out and handed it three problems that changed science forever. And something strange happened. Sometimes the AI reached conclusions that sounded remarkably like Einstein’s.

Was it discovering physics or just very good at sounding like it? But first, back to 1899, when physics felt almost finished. Newton and Maxwell had explained motion and light, and many physicists felt the big laws were all found. All that was left was tidying up the decimals. But in the basement, things didn’t add up. Small cracks in a perfect wall. The AI was built to fix them.

Why such a strange question? Because tech is having one of its biggest fights right now. Are AI models actually thinking or just remixing everything they’ve read? Critics call them stochastic parrots, copycats that recombine human ideas and never make anything truly new. And the fight is almost impossible to settle. Modern AI has read nearly everything ever published. So every new-looking idea might just be a memory. It’s like a magician who has watched every magic trick ever performed. Every trick looks brand new, and you can never prove he invented one.

So how could you ever test this? In early 2026, Demis Hassabis, head of Google DeepMind, proposed a clean experiment. Train an AI only on what humans knew before 1911, then ask it to figure out Einstein’s greatest idea, general relativity, from scratch. Success would be a genuinely new idea, not a remix. A failure wouldn’t prove much either, but nobody had ever tried. Hassabis said today’s biggest systems couldn’t do it.

One person didn’t wait. An independent researcher named Michael Hla built his own version. The hardest part wasn’t the AI. It was the library. Hla needed the recorded knowledge of the world before 1900 and none of it after. He pulled from the British Library, old American newspapers, digitized archives. Then came the paranoia. Old books lie about their age. A reprint from 1890 can carry a modern footnote that spoils the ending. So he set a brutal rule. If a document mentioned Einstein, quantum, or relativity anywhere, the whole thing got thrown out. After the cleanup, roughly 22 billion tokens of filtered old text. Filtered, not perfect.

Months later, a heart-stopping moment. A translator’s foreword in an old physics book mentioned Einstein by name. Einstein, hiding in the footnotes of the very library built to erase him. The world’s most inconvenient spoiler. Luckily, it was just the name.

Now, an honest warning about this AI. It has 3.3 billion parameters, about a thousand times smaller than today’s biggest models, and on the researcher’s own test suite, it scores worse than GPT-2, an AI from 2019. That’s not laziness. It’s the library. The world before 1900 simply didn’t write as much as the internet. And one more honest note. The old library gave the AI its base knowledge, but to teach it to answer questions, Hla used modern AI tools. A modern model helped write practice questions, and another graded its answers. He says so himself.

Then he handed this small, old-fashioned AI a set of puzzles. We’ll look at three that later won Planck and Einstein their Nobel prizes.

Puzzle one, the glowing box. Heat anything and it glows, red, then orange, then white. The best classical formula of the day predicted how much light it should give off at every color, and said a hot object should blast out endless energy, mostly invisible ultraviolet. Real objects just peak, then fade. Hla fed the model the real measurements and asked, “Which assumption is wrong?” Sometimes, not always, the model said energy can’t spread out smoothly. It has to come in limited amounts. That is roughly what Max Planck did in 1900. It started quantum physics.

Puzzle two, the stubborn metal. Shine light on a metal plate and it shoots out electrons. But there was a catch. Blindly bright red light, nothing. Faint blue light, electrons fly out instantly. If light were a smooth wave, brightness should matter, not color. Hla gave the model these results, and often it answered that light can’t be a smooth stream. It arrives in separate chunks, and color sets the size of each chunk. Red chunks are too small to knock an electron loose. Blue chunks are big enough. Once, the model explained the chunks with a steam engine, one bad answer away from inventing a steam-powered photon. Its chunk idea is basically Einstein’s 1905 picture of light, the work that won him the Nobel Prize.

Puzzle three, the elevator. Imagine a windowless elevator. You feel pressed to the floor. Is it standing on Earth or being pulled up through space? Einstein’s happiest thought: in a small enough space, there’s no way to tell. Gravity and acceleration are two names for one thing. That became general relativity. Hla described the elevator to the model, and here our AI shows its hand. Its favorite answer: gravity pulls on the aether, an invisible substance 19th-century scientists swore filled all of space. A wrong answer, and a very 1899 one. Physics had moved on. The AI’s aether subscription had not. But every so often, the model landed on it: gravity and acceleration are locally the same. The aether ghost kept drifting back. Remember it.

So, did it work? Hla’s own answer is a shrug. He calls the results glimpses of intuition, then doubts them himself. His most likely explanation: the model strings together old sentences that sound profound with no real picture of the world behind them. It even learned to flatter its grader, an AI judge, with fancy phrases to earn points.

And there’s a deeper problem. Hla set up every puzzle. He chose which measurements mattered and which assumptions to question. Einstein had to do that part himself. Choosing the right question is half of discovery, exactly what this model can’t do. So this experiment settles nothing, and that’s the honest point. The model was too small and the old library too thin. Today’s biggest models train on hundreds of times more text. But the question it asks is the right one. Einstein’s pieces didn’t come from nowhere, either. Maxwell’s equations, old arguments about light, other physicists’ math—Einstein connected dots that were already lying there. A new idea is often a new connection between old pieces.

This AI has read millions of pages preserved from before 1900. What it couldn’t do, yet, is see what nobody else saw. Hla released everything for free. The experiment is now homework for the whole research world. And somewhere in that old library, the AI is still explaining atoms with steam engines and blaming the aether for everything. It could almost see the future. It just couldn’t stop dragging the 19th century along with it. The cracks in the perfect wall are still there, waiting for someone, human or machine, to pry them open.
