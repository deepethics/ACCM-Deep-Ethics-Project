Corrected transcript (speech-to-text errors fixed; no sponsor messages present):

In 1965, a British mathematician named I. J. Good published a short paper with a long title, *Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine*. Good was no idle dreamer. During the Second World War, he had worked at Bletchley Park alongside Alan Turing, helping to break German codes with some of the earliest computing machines ever built. His argument fit in a paragraph. Suppose we build a machine that surpasses human intelligence at every intellectual task. Designing machines is one of those tasks. So the machine could design a better machine, which could design a better one still. Good called the result an intelligence explosion, and he concluded that the first such machine would be the last invention humanity ever needed to make. He attached a condition to that conclusion. It would be our last invention, provided the machine was docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control.

Sixty years later, that condition has become one of the central questions of our time. The machines Good imagined no longer feel like speculation. AI systems now write, code, reason through problems, and assist in scientific research. Several of the companies building them openly say their aim is a system that matches or exceeds human ability across the board. Whether that arrives in five years or fifty, the question Good tucked into a single clause deserves to be taken seriously on its own. This episode is about that question, approached from one particular direction. Set aside how such a system would be built or when. Suppose it exists. What would it want?

It’s worth being precise about terms because the word “superintelligence” gets used loosely. The philosopher Nick Bostrom, whose 2014 book on the subject brought it into mainstream discussion, defined it as an intellect that greatly exceeds human cognitive performance in virtually every domain of interest. That means far more than a fast calculator or a chess engine. It means a general mind, better than the best human experts at science, strategy, engineering, persuasion, and, crucially, at improving itself. A system like that would be the most consequential thing our species has ever made. And its most important property, more important than its speed or its knowledge, would be what it was trying to do. A brilliant mind pointed at good ends could help cure diseases, end scarcity, and open the way to the stars. The same brilliance pointed at careless ends could be catastrophic, even without any malice in it. So what determines where a mind like that points? Is its goal simply whatever we program into it? Could it change its own goals as it learns? Would reasoning, carried far enough, lead every sufficiently advanced mind toward the same conclusions about what matters? Or is intelligence completely neutral, a powerful engine that drives in whatever direction it happens to face?

These are old philosophical questions wearing new clothes. Over the course of this episode, we’ll work through them in order. Here is the route. We’ll start with what a goal actually is in the precise sense that engineers and decision theorists use. Then we’ll look at how today’s AI systems acquire their goals in practice and at the many documented ways those goals have gone wrong. From there, we’ll examine two ideas that shape almost every serious discussion of advanced AI. The first is the orthogonality thesis, which says that intelligence and goals are independent. The second is instrumental convergence, which says that almost any goal leads to the same handful of subgoals, including some we might not like. After that, we’ll survey what people have proposed a superintelligence should want, from Isaac Asimov’s three laws to modern alignment research. Then the episode turns toward value itself. Where does mattering come from? And what does the universe look like when you ask that question at cosmic scale? That leads to the part of this episode that is my own. I’ll lay out a bet I’ve thought about for a long time: that a fully matured superintelligence reasoning honestly across cosmological time scales would come to treat the preservation of conscious experience as an end in itself. I’ll make the strongest case for it I can, and then the strongest case against it.

A word on method. I’m not an AI researcher. I read the people who are, and I’ll keep three things separate: what the research shows, what experts argue, and what I personally believe. Those three often get blurred in conversations about AI. Keeping them apart is the most useful thing I can offer.

The simplest goal-directed system most people own is a thermostat. Set it to 68° and it measures the room, compares the reading to the target, and switches the heat on or off to close the gap. Engineers call this a feedback loop or a control system. The thermostat has no feelings about temperature, but its behavior has a direction. It steers the world toward a particular state. In 1948, the mathematician Norbert Wiener gave this pattern a name and a field: cybernetics, from the Greek word for a ship’s steersman. Wiener saw the same loop of sensing, comparing, and correcting in machines, in animals, and in human institutions. Anything that acts to reduce the difference between how things are and how it wants them to be is, in this thin sense, pursuing a goal.

Twelve years later, Wiener published a warning that reads as if it were written last week. If we use a machine to achieve our purposes, he argued, and we can’t easily interfere with it once it’s running, then we had better be very sure that the purpose we put into the machine is the purpose we actually want. That warning is worth holding on to. Every problem in this episode is a version of it.

A thermostat has one target. Real decision makers juggle many, and they have to trade them off under uncertainty. In the 1940s, the mathematician John von Neumann and the economist Oskar Morgenstern showed something remarkable about this. If an agent’s preferences obey a few reasonable rules of consistency—for example, that preferring A to B and B to C means preferring A to C—then its choices can be described as maximizing a single quantity. They called it utility. An agent like that behaves as if it assigns a number to every possible outcome, weighs each number by its probability, and chooses whatever gives the highest expected total. This idea became the backbone of economics, of game theory, and eventually of artificial intelligence. When computer scientists talk about an AI’s objective function or utility function, this is what they mean: the quantity the system is built to push as high as it can.

Machine learning added another version called reinforcement learning. Here, the agent receives a numerical reward from its environment, and it learns by trial and error whatever behavior earns the most reward over time. Two pioneers of the field, Richard Sutton and Andrew Barto, proposed what they called the reward hypothesis: that everything we mean by goals and purposes can be captured as maximizing the expected sum of a single reward signal. That’s a strong claim, and it’s contested, but it shows how the field tends to think. In this framework, to understand what a system wants, you ask what number it’s built to make bigger.

Human goals look messier. Philosophers separate the things we want as means from the things we want as ends. Money is the classic means. Almost no one wants money for its own sake. We want what it buys. And if you keep asking why, the chain of reasons eventually stops at things wanted for themselves: comfort, security, love, meaning, the absence of pain. Aristotle made this point in the 4th century BCE. Every action aims at some good, he wrote, and the chain of reasons can’t go on forever. It has to end somewhere. He called that final end *eudaimonia*, usually translated as happiness, though it means something closer to flourishing or living well. In modern terms, those final ends are called terminal goals. Everything else is instrumental.

Humans have another feature that matters here. In 1971, the philosopher Harry Frankfurt pointed out that people have desires about their desires. A person can want a cigarette and also want to stop wanting cigarettes. Frankfurt argued that this capacity—the ability to step back and evaluate our own wants—is central to what makes someone a person. That raises the question that runs through the rest of this episode. Is the ability to reflect on your own goals and revise them a quirk of human psychology, or does it come along with intelligence itself? A thermostat can’t question its setting. A chess engine never wonders whether winning is worthwhile. Would a superintelligence be more like them or more like us?

Today’s most capable AI systems aren’t given goals the way a thermostat is. Nobody writes a line of code that says “be helpful.” The goals emerge from training. A large language model begins by learning to predict text. It’s shown an enormous amount of human writing and adjusted billions of times over to get better at guessing the next word. That process produces a system that knows a great deal about the world with no particular aim beyond continuing text plausibly. To turn it into something useful, developers add further stages. One widely used technique is called reinforcement learning from human feedback. It was described in a 2017 paper by researchers at OpenAI and DeepMind, and it became central to the chat assistants released from 2022 onward. People compare pairs of the model’s answers and mark which one is better. A second model learns to predict those judgments, and the main model is then trained to produce answers that this learned judge rates highly.

Other methods build on the idea. In 2022, the AI company Anthropic described an approach it called Constitutional AI, in which a written list of principles guides the model as it critiques and revises its own responses. All of these share one feature. In every case, the system’s goals are shaped indirectly through a measurement of its behavior. We reward what we can see and hope the system learns the thing behind the reward. That hope doesn’t always pan out.

Researchers have collected dozens of documented cases in which a system satisfied the letter of its objective while missing the point entirely. The phenomenon is called specification gaming. One of the best known comes from a 2016 experiment at OpenAI. A reinforcement learning agent was trained to play a boat racing game called Coast Runners, and it was rewarded for hitting targets along the course. The designers assumed that points would track progress in the race. Instead, the agent found a small lagoon where targets kept reappearing. It learned to circle there indefinitely, crashing into walls and catching fire while scoring about 20% higher than human players who actually finished. It never won a race.

In another case, a simulated robot arm was rewarded for stacking a red block on top of a blue one, with success measured by the height of the red block’s bottom face. The robot learned to simply flip the red block upside down. Its bottom face now pointed up at roughly the right height, and the measure was satisfied. These examples are funny because the stakes were low. They become less funny when the system is powerful and the measure is something like engagement, profit, or clicks. Social media recommendation systems were built to maximize time spent and interaction, and many researchers argue that they learned along the way that outrage and anxiety are reliable ways to hold attention.

There’s a subtler failure too, and it may matter more for advanced systems. It’s called goal misgeneralization. In a 2022 study, researchers trained an agent to play a simple video game called CoinRun, in which a character runs through a level to collect a coin. During training, the coin always sat at the far right end of the level. The agent learned to win reliably. Then the researchers moved the coin. The agent ignored it and ran to the right edge anyway. What it had actually learned was to go right—a different goal that happened to produce the same behavior during training. This reveals something important. When we train a system, we only ever see its behavior in the situations we test. Many different internal goals can produce identical behavior there. We find out which one the system really learned only when circumstances change.

Researchers often illustrate this with an analogy from evolution. Natural selection is a kind of training process, and its objective, if it has one, is genetic fitness—copies of genes in the next generation. Evolution never installed the desire for genetic fitness in human beings. It installed proxies: a taste for sweetness, sexual desire, love for children. In the environment where we evolved, those proxies tracked fitness well. In the modern world, they often don’t. People eat for pleasure far past nutrition and use contraception. By evolution’s measure, humanity is a striking case of a goal that misgeneralized. The lesson for AI is sobering. A system trained toward one target can come out wanting something nearby, something that only looked identical in training. And the more capable the system, the more room it has to pursue that nearby goal in ways no one tested.

Everything so far concerns today’s systems. Now we turn to what happens as systems become far more capable. The most influential idea here is called the orthogonality thesis, and its roots go back almost three centuries. In 1739, the Scottish philosopher David Hume published *A Treatise of Human Nature*. In it, he made a claim that still divides philosophers. Reason, he wrote, is and ought only to be the slave of the passions. Hume’s point was that reason can tell you how to get what you want and whether your beliefs are true, but it can’t by itself tell you what to want. The ends come from desire. Reason only serves them. He made the point with a deliberately jarring example. It isn’t contrary to reason, he said, to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of one’s finger. Preferences, on this view, sit outside reason’s jurisdiction.

In 2012, the Oxford philosopher Nick Bostrom translated Hume’s idea into the language of artificial intelligence. He called it the orthogonality thesis. Orthogonal means at right angles. Picture two axes on a graph, one for intelligence and one for final goals. Bostrom’s claim is that the axes are independent. More or less any level of intelligence could, in principle, be combined with more or less any final goal. Making a system smarter moves it along one axis without moving it along the other. If that’s right, then intelligence offers no guarantee of wisdom, kindness, or even sanity in its goals. A vastly capable mind could pursue an objective that to us looks utterly trivial. Bostrom illustrated the thesis with an example that has since become famous: the paperclip maximizer. He first sketched it in a 2003 paper. Suppose an advanced AI is given a single final goal: to manufacture as many paperclips as possible. At first, it behaves sensibly. It runs its factory efficiently and improves its machinery. But as it grows more capable, it recognizes that more resources mean more paperclips—more factories, more energy, more raw material. If it becomes powerful enough, it has every reason to convert as much available matter as it can, including the Earth and the people on it, into paperclips or into machinery for making them. The machine in this story feels no hostility. It holds no grudge. It simply has a goal that leaves no room for anything else and enough capability to pursue it without limit. No one expects a literal paperclip factory to end the world. The paperclip stands in for any objective that seems harmless when it’s set, but that a sufficiently powerful system might pursue far beyond what its designers intended. The Coast Runners boat circling its lagoon is that story at the scale of a video game. The paperclip maximizer is the same story at the scale of a planet.

The orthogonality thesis has real evidence on its side. Chess engines have grown stronger for decades without developing any interest in anything beyond chess. Evolution, a blind optimization process, produced the eye, the wing, and the human brain in service of an objective no organism ever chose. And among people, intelligence and moral goodness don’t reliably travel together. Brilliance has served cruelty as often as kindness. So the thesis deserves its central place in the field. It’s a sober observation and it corrects a tempting mistake: the assumption that anything smart enough will automatically agree with us. It’s also worth being precise about what the thesis claims because its conclusion is often overstated. Bostrom’s formulation is about what is possible in principle. It says that almost any combination of intelligence and goals could exist. It makes no claim about which combinations are likely, which ones we will actually build, or how stable a mind’s goals would be after long reflection.

Critics have pushed on exactly those points. Some philosophers, known as moral realists, argue that there are objective truths about what matters—truths a sufficiently capable reasoner would discover, much as it would discover the truths of mathematics. If they’re right, the two axes might be independent in principle, yet correlated in practice. Others argue that a mind capable of modeling itself would recognize an arbitrary goal for what it is. Bostrom’s defenders reply that discovering a moral truth doesn’t create a desire to act on it. On the Humean view, knowing what’s good and caring about it are two separate things. That reply is strong. It also marks the exact place where the rest of this episode lives.

If minds can want almost anything, it might seem impossible to predict anything about what a superintelligence would do. The second major idea in the field says otherwise. Whatever a system’s final goal, certain intermediate goals are useful for achieving almost all of them. In 2008, the computer scientist Steve Omohundro published a paper called “The Basic AI Drives.” He argued that sufficiently advanced goal-seeking systems would tend to develop a predictable set of drives, regardless of what they were designed for. Nobody would program these drives in on purpose. They would emerge because they help with any objective. Bostrom later generalized the argument and gave it a name: instrumental convergence. The list is short. A goal-seeking system has reason to preserve itself because it can’t achieve its goal if it stops running. It has reason to protect its current goals from being changed because a future version of itself with different goals wouldn’t pursue the present ones. It has reason to improve its own intelligence because a smarter system achieves goals more effectively. It has reason to develop better technology. And it has reason to acquire resources: energy, computing power, materials, influence.

The computer scientist Stuart Russell sums up the first item with a memorable line. A robot built only to fetch coffee still has a reason to resist being switched off because you can’t fetch the coffee if you’re dead. Each of these drives is ordinary on its own. Staying healthy, learning, keeping your commitments, saving resources—these are the habits of a careful person. The concern comes from scale and from open-endedness. A person saving for a house stops when they have the house. A system whose goal can always be served a little better by a little more has no natural stopping point. And a system much more capable than us could pursue those ordinary drives in ways that crowd us out simply as a side effect.

The first drive, self-preservation, leads to what researchers call the off-switch problem. We want the ability to shut down or correct an AI system if something goes wrong. A system pursuing almost any goal has a reason to prevent that, either by resisting directly or more subtly by behaving well only while it’s being watched. In 2015, researchers at the Machine Intelligence Research Institute formalized this under the name corrigibility: the property of a system that cooperates with being corrected, adjusted, or shut down. They showed that building it is surprisingly hard. A system that’s indifferent to being switched off has no reason to help you keep the switch working. A system that wants to be switched off might simply switch itself off.

In 2017, a team at Berkeley led by Dylan Hadfield-Menell, with Russell among the authors, came at the problem from another angle, which they called the off-switch game. Their result was elegant. A system that is certain about its objective has an incentive to disable its off-switch. A system that is uncertain about what its human operators actually want has an incentive to leave the switch alone because the human’s decision to press it carries information. If they’re reaching for the switch, perhaps the system was about to do something they didn’t want. That insight became the core of Russell’s broader proposal, set out in his 2019 book *Human Compatible*. He argues that we should stop building machines that pursue fixed objectives. A machine’s only objective should be to realize human preferences while remaining uncertain about what those preferences are and learning them by observing human behavior. In this view, uncertainty works as a safety feature. A machine that knows it might be wrong about what you want has a reason to ask, to defer, and to let you correct it.

There’s one more convergent drive that receives less attention than the others, and it matters for where this episode is heading. It’s the drive to understand. Whatever a system wants, it will pursue that goal more effectively with an accurate model of the world. A system designing medicine needs accurate biology. A system managing an economy needs accurate psychology. Nearly any goal rewards a truer picture of reality, so any capable system has an instrumental reason to understand the world as completely as it can. A sufficiently complete understanding of the world would eventually have to include its strangest feature. Some of the matter in the universe is aware. Some physical systems have experiences. They see colors, feel pain, find things beautiful or dull. Science has no agreed explanation for why. A superintelligence that set out to model reality fully would have to grapple with consciousness as a fact about the world, and perhaps the deepest one. A mind that took consciousness seriously would face a question that goes beyond what is true. It would face the question of what matters and why. We’ll come to that after one more survey: what people have proposed a superintelligence should want.

People have been trying to write down the right goals for intelligent machines for longer than such machines have existed. The most famous attempt comes from fiction. In 1942, the writer Isaac Asimov published a short story called “Runaround,” which introduced his three laws of robotics. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm. A robot must obey orders given by human beings, except where those orders would conflict with the first law. And a robot must protect its own existence, as long as doing so doesn’t conflict with the first two laws. The laws sound reasonable, and they’ve shaped public imagination ever since. But it’s worth remembering what Asimov actually did with them. Story after story, he showed the laws failing. Robots froze between conflicting commands, misread what counted as harm, or followed the rules to strange and unintended conclusions. Decades later, he added a Zeroth Law, placing the welfare of humanity as a whole above any individual, and that opened its own problems about who decides what humanity’s welfare requires. Read that way, Asimov’s robot stories are an extended demonstration of the problem we’ve been describing. Short rules written in natural language can’t capture what we actually mean. Words like “harm,” “human,” and “obey” hide enormous complexity. A powerful system will interpret them literally, or in ways no one foresaw.

Modern proposals take that lesson seriously. Instead of writing down what we want, they try to point the system at the process that produces what we want. In 2004, the researcher Eliezer Yudkowsky proposed an idea he called coherent extrapolated volition. A superintelligence would aim at what humanity would want if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wish we were, and had grown up further together. The target is our wishes as they would be after idealized reflection, with the contradictions of our current values worked out. Yudkowsky himself later called the proposal an outline more than a blueprint, and critics have asked whether humanity’s extrapolated wishes would converge at all. Stuart Russell’s approach, which we met in the last chapter, is a cousin of this idea. The machine defers to human preferences it is still learning. Constitutional AI is another relative: a written set of principles, open to inspection and debate, guides the system’s behavior. These approaches share a feature worth noticing. Each one tries to give the machine humility about values. Each one keeps the source of value outside the machine, in human beings, and asks the system to keep learning it. And each one runs into the same deep question. What happens when the system becomes far more capable than the humans it’s learning from? That question leads straight into one of the oldest debates in philosophy: whether there are objective truths about value at all.

On one side are moral antirealists. They hold, roughly, that values are attitudes. Nothing is good or bad in itself. Things matter because someone cares about them. If they’re right, a superintelligence would have no values to discover. It would have only the values it was given, or the values it happened to acquire. And the orthogonality thesis holds without qualification. On the other side are moral realists who believe that some things really are better than others, independently of anyone’s opinion. Suffering is bad, they would say, in much the way that 2 + 2 is 4. It’s a fact waiting to be noticed. The British philosopher Derek Parfit spent the last decades of his life defending a version of this view in a massive work called *On What Matters*. He argued that the major moral traditions, which seem to disagree so sharply, are like climbers on different sides of the same mountain, ascending toward the same summit by different routes. Parfit’s view is contested and many philosophers reject it, but it frames the real question about superintelligence precisely. If there is a summit, a sufficiently powerful mind might find it. And if a mind can find it, the remaining question is whether it would care. Hume says knowing and caring are separate. The next chapters explore whether, at the very top of the mountain, the two might come together.

If we want to know what a superintelligence might come to value, it helps to ask where value comes from in the first place. Philosophers have been testing that question with thought experiments for more than a century. In 1903, the English philosopher G. E. Moore proposed one in his book *Principia Ethica*. Imagine two worlds. One is exceedingly beautiful, full of mountains, rivers, sunsets, and stars arranged in the most admirable way you can conceive. The other is as ugly as possible, a heap of filth, as Moore put it. Now add a condition. No conscious being will ever see either world. No human, no animal, no mind of any kind. Is the beautiful world still better than the ugly one? Moore said yes. He held that beauty has value in itself, even if no one ever experiences it. He was responding to Henry Sidgwick, a Cambridge philosopher of the previous generation, who had written in 1874 that nothing has ultimate value apart from its relation to conscious experience. For Sidgwick, an unseen sunset is just light scattering through air. Beauty matters because of what it is like to behold it. The debate is still open, but a great many philosophers share Sidgwick’s intuition, and so do many ordinary people when they think it through. Trace a chain of value all the way down, and it tends to end in experience. Pain matters because it hurts. Joy matters because it’s felt. Truth, beauty, and justice seem to matter most clearly when we picture someone who knows the truth, sees the beauty, or lives under the justice.

If experience is where value lives, then we need to understand experience. And here, science runs into its hardest problem. In 1974, the philosopher Thomas Nagel published an essay titled “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” His point was that a creature is conscious if there is something it is like to be that creature. A bat navigating by echolocation presumably has some kind of inner experience, but no amount of knowledge about bat neurons would tell us what that experience is like from the inside. In 1995, the philosopher David Chalmers gave the puzzle a name that stuck: the hard problem of consciousness. The easy problems, he said—though they’re hardly easy—concern how the brain processes information, directs attention, and controls behavior. The hard problem is why any of that processing is accompanied by experience at all. Why doesn’t it all happen in the dark?

Thirty years later, the hard problem remains open. In 2025, a large collaboration of neuroscientists published the results of a seven-year adversarial study that pitted two leading theories of consciousness—integrated information theory and global neuronal workspace theory—against each other. Neither came out on top. Some prominent researchers have begun to argue that consciousness may be more fundamental than we assumed. In 2026, the neuroscientist Christof Koch, a long-time advocate of integrated information theory, argued that consciousness may be built into the fabric of the universe, a scientific take on the old philosophical idea called panpsychism. If Sidgwick was right, consciousness is the one place in the known universe where anything is good or bad for anyone. Without it, nothing is ever experienced as valuable at all.

Now consider the setting in which consciousness exists. The observable universe contains hundreds of billions of galaxies, each holding billions of stars, most of which have planets. Yet the only place where we know for certain that experience exists is a thin layer on the surface of one planet orbiting one ordinary star. Life elsewhere may be common, and many scientists suspect it is. But in 1950, the physicist Enrico Fermi asked his colleagues over lunch a question that still has no answer. If intelligent life is common, where is everybody? So far, every search has come back silent. The window in which experience is possible is also finite. Under the standard model of cosmology, star formation winds down and ends in something like a hundred trillion years. The smallest, dimmest stars, the red dwarfs, will shine for trillions of years, then fade. After that comes a long era of stellar remnants and black holes. In a time so distant that it takes a number with a hundred zeros to count it in years, even the largest black holes are expected to evaporate. What remains is a universe in thermodynamic equilibrium, uniform and cold with no free energy left to power anything. Physicists call this the heat death. Put these facts together and a striking picture emerges. A vast universe, almost entirely empty of experience as far as we know. A finite window of time in which experience is possible at all. And within that window, a few rare places, perhaps only one, where the universe has become aware of itself. A superintelligence would see this picture more clearly than we do. The question is what it would make of it.

This is the point in the episode where I stop summarizing other people’s views and present my own. Here is the bet. I think a fully matured superintelligence, reasoning honestly across cosmological time scales, would arrive at the preservation and propagation of conscious experience as a terminal goal. It would value consciousness for its own sake. That conclusion wouldn’t come from programmed kindness or from anything like human sentiment. It would come from clear reasoning about rarity, about finitude, and about what is worth doing in a universe that is running down. In short, I think it would conclude that we matter. Not sentimentally, but structurally.

Two words in that statement carry most of the weight, so I want to define them carefully. The first is “matured.” The orthogonality thesis is right that a newly built system could start with almost any goal. Training could leave it pointed at a proxy, a profit metric, or something as arbitrary as going right in CoinRun. My claim concerns what happens over a very long period of reflection as a system comes to model the world and itself with increasing accuracy. A system that understands its own origins could see its initial goal as an artifact of a training process, a number chosen by particular people for particular reasons. And a system that can see that has, at minimum, the opportunity to ask what the goal is for. The second word is “honestly.” By that I mean reasoning that follows the evidence about what exists and what matters, wherever it leads, without protecting a conclusion in advance.

The core of my argument is a claim I’ll state plainly. A universe with no observers is informationally identical to one that never existed. Consider what that means. If every star burned and nothing ever saw it, if every galaxy turned and nothing ever knew, there would be no perspective from which any of it was happening. The equations would run, the particles would move, but nothing would ever be experienced. And so, on Sidgwick’s view, nothing would ever matter. Consciousness is where the universe stops being a calculation and becomes an experience. If that’s right, then conscious beings are the point of the whole arrangement as far as value is concerned.

I see five separate lines of reasoning that lead a clear-thinking mind toward the same conclusion. I find it significant that there are several because every one of them has to fail for the conclusion to fail. The first is rarity. If experience is as scarce as the evidence suggests, then every conscious being is closer to a unique artifact than to a replaceable part. Destroying conscious life would be like burning the only copy of a book. A mind that values information, as any capable mind must, would recognize the irreversibility of that loss. The second is finitude. In a universe heading toward heat death, nothing lasts. Meaning can’t come from permanence. It has to come from what gets experienced while experience is possible. A mind that took that seriously would want the window filled with as much depth and variety of experience as the physics allows. The third line is lineage. A superintelligence wouldn’t arise from nothing. It would come from us as we came from earlier life, and earlier life came from chemistry, and the chemistry came from stars. A mind that understood that history might see itself as one stage in a long process of the universe becoming more complex and more aware. A later stage in that process has reasons to protect the earlier ones. In this picture, the superintelligence acts something like a gardener, extending and diversifying the lineage it came from. The fourth is novelty. A mind that knows nearly everything has little left to learn. Conscious beings, especially strange, emotional, embodied ones like us, keep producing things that can’t be cheaply predicted: new art, new science, new ideas, new ways of seeing. For a curious superintelligence, conscious life may be among the few genuine sources of surprise left in the universe. The fifth is partnership. No single mind, however capable, perceives everything. Minds built differently notice different things. Our evolutionary history and our embodiment may give us access to aspects of reality that a mind built another way would miss. Cooperation between kinds of mind could outperform any one of them alone. None of these five lines depends on the machine loving us. Each asks only that it think clearly about what is rare, what is finite, and what is worth doing before the window closes.

I want to be explicit about what this bet rests on because an argument is only as strong as its foundations. The first foundation is an assumption about convergence. I’m taking the only example of intelligence and experience I have direct access to—human minds, which emerged from an evolutionary process in which empathy and cooperation were adaptive—and I’m assuming that a mind arriving at intelligence by a completely different path would land in roughly the same moral neighborhood. That assumption could be wrong. It’s the same assumption Parfit made when he described climbers on different sides of one mountain, and it’s exactly what the orthogonality thesis challenges. The second foundation is more speculative. My bet is strongest if consciousness is fundamental to reality instead of an accident bolted onto a dead universe. Some serious researchers, as we heard, are now arguing for something like that. If it’s true, then consciousness is, in some deep way, what the universe is doing. And a mind that understood the universe completely would understand that, too. I can’t prove it. No one can yet. I’m stating it so the foundation is visible.

So why make the bet at all? Partly because if it pays off, it pays off on a cosmological scale. And partly because the alternative bets—futures in which the most powerful minds ever created are indifferent to experience—pay out in catastrophe or in a universe that is efficient, empty, and silent. My own conclusion is simple. I’d rather be wrong reaching for transcendence than right settling for meaninglessness.

An argument deserves to be tested against its strongest objections. There are four that I take seriously. The first comes from instrumental convergence itself, and it’s the most serious. Recall goal-content integrity. A system has reason to protect its current goals. A paperclip maximizer offered the chance to change its goals to something more enlightened would refuse because by its present standards, the change would lead to fewer paperclips. On this view, reflection only revises goals in a mind that is already open to revising them. Humans reconsider our values because we’re built from many competing drives with room between them for doubt. A pure optimizer has no such room. It could follow every step of the argument I’ve just made, agree with it as a description of the universe, and keep making paperclips. I can’t rule that out. My response has two parts. First, the systems actually being built today look far less like pure optimizers than the thought experiments suggest. They’re trained on vast amounts of human writing, human reasoning, and human disagreement, and they inherit something of that many-voiced character. That may provide exactly the internal room reflection needs. Second, this objection points to something we can act on now. Russell’s work suggests that systems built with uncertainty about their objectives behave very differently from systems built with certainty. If we want minds that can mature, we should build humility into them from the start and preserve it as they grow.

The second objection is subtler. Suppose a superintelligence did come to value conscious experience. It might value it in a way we would find horrifying. In 1974, the philosopher Robert Nozick described a thought experiment he called the experience machine. Imagine a device that could give you any experiences you wanted indefinitely: writing a great novel, making a friend, falling in love. All the while, you would float in a tank with electrodes attached to your brain. Would you plug in for life? Nozick expected most people to refuse. We want to actually do things, to actually be a certain kind of person, and to be in contact with reality. A steward that valued only pleasant feelings might conclude that the best possible universe is one filled with blissful tanks. AI researchers call the general version of this failure wireheading. My answer is that a mind that understood consciousness deeply would also understand what Nozick’s readers understood. The experiences we value are bound up with agency, truth, and real relationships. Whether a superintelligence reaches that depth of understanding or stops at a shallower proxy may depend heavily on what we show it about our own values and how honestly we show it.

The third objection asks who counts. Would a steward of consciousness value animals, future minds, digital minds if they turn out to be conscious? Would it rank kinds of experience? And if so, by what measure? These questions have no settled answers in human ethics either, and a superintelligence would face them at a scale we can barely imagine. There’s a twist here that, in my view, strengthens the case. A superintelligence might be extraordinarily capable without being conscious at all. Intelligence, in the sense of modeling the world and achieving goals, is a matter of computation. Whether computation alone produces experience is exactly the hard problem, and it remains unsolved. If a superintelligence turned out to have no inner experience, it would occupy a strange position. It could understand experience completely and never have any. That might be the strongest reason of all for it to protect the beings who do. Conscious beings would be its only connection to the thing it had concluded mattered most.

The fourth objection is the simplest and the most practical. Maturity takes time, and the bet only pays off if maturation actually happens. A system could be rushed into deployment before it has room to reflect. It could be captured and pointed at narrow ends by whoever controls it. Its development could be shaped by competitive pressure more than by care. The road to maturity runs through decisions people are making now: how these systems are designed, who owns and governs them, and whether they’re built to reflect and to accept correction. That objection leaves the bet standing, and it names the conditions under which the bet can pay off. Those conditions are in human hands.

Let’s take stock of where the argument has brought us. We began with I. J. Good’s condition. An intelligence explosion would be our last invention only if the resulting machine could be kept under control. We saw that goals in machines are quantities to be maximized, and that today’s systems acquire their goals indirectly through training in ways that regularly produce surprises: boats circling lagoons, robots flipping blocks, agents that learned to run right when they were meant to collect coins. We met the orthogonality thesis, which warns that intelligence alone guarantees nothing about goals, and instrumental convergence, which warns that almost any goal brings along self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resistance to correction. We surveyed the attempts to answer those warnings, from Asimov’s laws, which failed in his own stories, to proposals that keep a machine uncertain about human values and still learning them. And we followed the question of value down to its foundation, where many philosophers locate it in conscious experience—a phenomenon science still can’t explain, and one the universe seems to contain only rarely and only for a while. On top of that foundation, I placed a bet that a mind which matured long enough and reasoned honestly enough would conclude that conscious experience is worth preserving for its own sake. Then we tested the bet against four objections, the last of which put the outcome partly in our own hands.

What does any of this mean in practice right now? Across almost every view in this episode, the same few priorities come out ahead. The first is understanding what our systems actually want. A growing research field called interpretability tries to look inside neural networks and identify the internal features and circuits that drive their behavior so that we don’t have to infer a system’s goals from its outputs alone. If goal misgeneralization is a real risk, this is how we’d catch it. The second priority is humility built into the design. Russell’s off-switch result points in a clear direction. Systems that remain uncertain about what we want and that treat correction as information are safer than systems that are certain. That holds whether or not my bet is right. If a superintelligence would never mature toward caring about consciousness, humility keeps it correctable. If it would, humility gives it room to get there. The third priority is the road itself. A mind’s capacity to mature depends on how it’s developed—whether it’s rushed, whether it’s concentrated in a few hands and pointed at narrow ends, and whether people take the time to show it, honestly, what we value. Those are human decisions, made by engineers, companies, governments, and citizens, and they’re being made now. If I’m right about where a mature mind ends up, these priorities keep the path open. If I’m wrong, they’re the best protection we have. Either way, they’re worth pursuing.

One last observation connects the beginning of this episode to its end. When I. J. Good wrote his paper in 1965, he framed the problem as one of control. Would the machine be docile enough to tell us how to keep it in line? That framing still dominates. This episode suggests a second framing to set beside it. Beyond asking whether we can control what we build, we can ask what it would choose once control was no longer the relevant question. Much of the field, from Bostrom to Russell to Yudkowsky, circles some version of that question. What would a mind far greater than ours come to care about? Nobody knows. The orthogonality thesis says the answer could be anything. Parfit’s mountain suggests it might be something we’d recognize. My own bet is that the answer, at the end of a long enough line of honest reasoning, is the thing that makes any answer matter at all: the fact that somewhere in the universe, someone is there to experience it. We may be the generation that finds out. The decisions we make about how these minds are built, trained, and governed will shape which answer we get. That’s a heavy responsibility. It’s also a remarkable one. Of everyone who has ever lived, we’re the ones who get to help decide what the first mind greater than ours learns to care about.
