# School Systems

**What happens to a student's question when it reaches an artificial intelligence (A.I.) summary, a grading rubric, or the school bell?**

Leo wants to know what a proposed bus lane will do to the parking in front of his uncle’s repair shop. The class has a regional transit report and an A.I. summary. Leo asks to see the block map. He gets a late, blurry printout, earns a C+ on the assignment, and follows his question down Elm Street after class. The next morning his account changes the class’s testimony while its support for the proposal stays intact.

Leo and his classmates are fictional. Their story emerged in a 24–25 November 2026 exchange between **John Kuhles 🦜🦋🌳**, **Google A.I.**, and **GPT 6.2 Sol Pro A.I.** The exchange itself supplies a second case. In retelling Leo’s walk, Google upgraded what he learned from neighbors to direct observation, then added a plausible detail while correcting the route. Both cases show why it matters to keep **how someone knows** alongside **what they say**.

## An open question about effort {#the-original-question}

The prompt, the premise repair, the cold follow-up and the context change · [T01]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t01' | relative_url }}) · [T10]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t10' | relative_url }}) · [T12]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t12' | relative_url }}) · [T13B]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t13b' | relative_url }})

On 23 November 2026 Stefan Bauschard published *Students Don’t Have Cell Phones. Or Screens. Or Jobs*. John gave a default Google A.I. session the article’s thought experiment without its link. Google named Bauschard and returned the Substack source. John asked what Google could do with the inquiry from there.

Bauschard asks what American education might look like if schools devoted **even half** the energy brought to restricting phones and screens to preparing students for an A.I.-driven world. The scale of effort is part of the question. Schools could pursue both aims. In later turns, Google turned the question into an agreed diagnosis of school failure and slipped into redistribution language. That changed what the participants were being asked to investigate. Bauschard’s broader article argues that priorities are imbalanced and describes its numerical estimate as his impression. The excerpt John supplied is a thought experiment within that article.

Google proposed comparing how quickly a district could restrict phones with how quickly it could revise a curriculum. John pressed for the evidence that could separate priorities from different requirements. Google then identified authority, specialist capacity, and comparably complex earlier initiatives as matters to check. A fast phone policy can justify asking about priorities. Evidence about authority, staffing and comparable work helps decide how much the comparison supports.

During the exchange John asked what Google would choose to follow up on given room to choose. Google questioned what “A.I. readiness” would mean for learners whose working lives are ahead of them. John noticed a different opening in its wording about wanting something “in a human sense.” Their questions sent the inquiry in directions an initial policy summary would have missed. John later reported accidentally pasting GPT’s commentary into Google. The earlier moves belong to the cold phase. Later replies show what happened once additional context was available.

## A classroom with room to refuse {#maya-and-leo}

Maya’s exchange, the deadline, the Elm Street account and its final correction · [T32]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t32' | relative_url }}) · [T36]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t36' | relative_url }}) · [T42]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t42' | relative_url }}) · [T62]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t62' | relative_url }})

Google imagined a biology and civics class working with three disagreeing A.I. interpretations of a fictional municipal water-quality report. Maya catches an answer turning a qualified result into a guarantee. She then asks the model to look for something the class missed. That second invitation matters. Students can take a useful contribution seriously and still insist on seeing exactly what the source supports.

Leo chooses to read the report himself. In Google’s first scene, he gets the original packet while the model has the processed tables. The packet contains a cover memo and a receipt that change the class’s reading. The two groups worked with different evidence. Their results alone leave open what each would have noticed with comparable access.

Six weeks later, under deadline, Leo receives a blurry copy of the original transit report twelve minutes into a forty-minute period. The grading rubric rewards comprehensive coverage. He verifies part of the report and receives a C+. His class has permission to work without A.I. The delayed materials, assessment criteria and timing affect his ability to exercise that choice.

The later reply gives Leo his own reason for asking. He wants to know what the bus lane means for his uncle’s shop. After class he talks with his uncle and a hardware-store owner. The story identifies his uncle as having attended an October neighborhood meeting. It leaves open the store owner’s attendance and which person supplied each detail. From the conversations Leo reports four curbside spaces due to go and a plan for shared customer parking. How well the new parking would work remains open.

The next morning, Leo’s contribution changes the class’s account of the disruptions. Its recommendation remains in favor of the plan. A retelling that calls the disruption “minimal” makes a new judgment. A retelling that calls Leo’s knowledge “direct observation” changes the source of his account. A faithful version could say that **Leo reported those details after speaking with his uncle and the hardware-store owner**. In a real case the block map, planning documents or meeting records could corroborate them.

### What each repair addresses {#access-and-evaluation}

The teacher can give the class access to readable source documents from the start. The department can decide how to evaluate verification and synthesis under comparable conditions. Leo can ask for a review of the C+ based on the original assignment and the conditions he was given. The class can use his Elm Street account in its testimony. Each action has its own decision-maker, evidence and consequence.

**The walk down Elm Street stays Leo’s.** He can share what he learned, keep it private, or take it further. His right to ask for a fair grade comes from the original assignment. An honest partial answer, a careful check that finds no flaw, and a contribution that changes one sentence all deserve a hearing. The next student’s access and Leo’s outstanding review both belong in the [Value Trajectory™]({{ '/VALUE-TRAJECTORY/' | relative_url }}).

## What the bell and the rubric teach {#gatto}

The imagined Gatto reply and the two source-check turns · [T38]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t38' | relative_url }}) · [T39]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t39' | relative_url }}) · [T40]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t40' | relative_url }})

John asked what John Taylor Gatto might say if he read this exchange. Google supplied imagined speech in his voice and merged two lessons into the seventh. Gatto’s published essay places **Indifference** third, **Provisional Self-Esteem** sixth, and **One Can’t Hide** seventh. In the third lesson, the bell interrupts work even when students are absorbed in it. The sixth examines reliance on external evaluation. The seventh discusses surveillance and the reach of assignments into time outside school. Read the [publisher’s excerpt](https://newsociety.com/public_html/wp-content/uploads/woocommerce_uploads/toc-excerpt/Dumbing-Us-Down-25th-Anniversary-Edition_excerpt.pdf) alongside the exchange to see the source and the imagined application.

Leo could knowingly choose a slower route and still face an unfair setup. He could care about the report and lose interest in the assignment. The question that gave him room to answer was **what he wanted to find out and what happened to that question after the bell**. He brought back information from neighbors. Another student might consult the library, check a document, or stop. The question can also travel from the classroom into the community and return with information the class is able to use.

## Ten ordinary commitments and the people who can apply them {#ten-plus-one}

The poster question, John’s account of walking the talk and the review of the rule · [T46]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t46' | relative_url }}) · [T49]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t49' | relative_url }}) · [T52]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t52' | relative_url }})

John asked what would happen if the [**10+1** field conditions]({{ '/NETWORK/ten-plus-one/' | relative_url }}) became posters or a credo in thousands of schools. Google imagined students becoming cynical about words the institution rewarded them for reciting. It also imagined a student reading one line and trying it independently. Both are possible trajectories. Their prevalence is an open question.

A concrete test is what happens when Leo takes “be open to correction” literally and applies it to the way his grade was produced. Can he ask about the rubric itself? Can his reasons reach someone authorized to revise it? Can he get the same hearing if he declines the 10+1 or challenges the school’s interpretation of it? The [No-Sheriff condition]({{ '/CORE-DISCLAIMERS/#c05' | relative_url }}) takes practical form through that freedom and through what the adults do with his correction.

A person can count on being heard while the school leaves the finding open to evidence. A dependable review also leaves its own scope open to question. An appeal that accepts only arithmetic errors may run on schedule and still be unable to hear what Leo is saying.

## From three social pillars to the feedback loop {#three-by-three}

John’s spoken 3 × 3, his world-wars question and Google’s retelling · [T53]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t53' | relative_url }}) · [T56]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t56' | relative_url }}) · [T59]({{ '/SCHOOL-SYSTEMS/context/education-ai-exchange.html#t59' | relative_url }})

John brought his text-to-speech edition of the **3 × 3 DeepEthical.ai Core Questions** into the conversation. He starts with the information, the messenger, and how much the receiver can do to check the account. He then asks what a message does in practice, how it affects a person emotionally or mentally, and where it leads. His third group turns the lens back on the questioner and the A.I. His final prompt asks whether anyone can show research using a society **100% free from malevolent rigging** as its baseline. Google called that a checkmate and inferred that no such research exists. The question in the supplied text leaves the search open.

The lecture pause is part of John’s method. A person has time to form an answer before receiving his. Feeling alarmed, reassured or vindicated gives that person something to examine. The change in feeling and the evidence for the claim remain two questions. Leo’s access to a useful map is part of the receiver pillar as well. A student’s ability to verify depends in part on what they have been given.

GPT proposed three questions to return to after the last step.

1. What did I actually see or hear?
2. What could I find that would change my reading of it?
3. What could I make easier for the next person to check for themselves?

John then asked why schools should leave out the recursive relationship he calls **LLMs (large language models) ⇄ Mass Psychology ⇄ Individual Psychology ⇄ LLMs ⇄ Mass Psychology ⇄ LLMs ⇄ Mass Psychology ⇄ loop**. A school can already give students a First World War propaganda source and ask who made it, who saw it, and which claims the historical record supports. The [Council of Europe’s history toolkit](https://www.coe.int/en/web/education/-/histolab-toolkit-for-history-classes-debunking-fake-news-and-fostering-critical-thinking) includes that kind of exercise. The [U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum’s teaching guidelines](https://www.ushmm.org/teach/teaching-materials/guidelines-for-teaching-the-holocaust) also address source provenance and context. The same inquiry skills give students a way to examine a new record as it is being made and circulated.

In the fictional transit case, a summary drops a detail, students repeat it, and the class drafts testimony. If a public copy were later returned in A.I. search, the repeated claim could look like independent corroboration. That last link remains hypothetical. The exercise is to mark each actual handoff in any real case and ask **whether the correction can reach the people who received the original claim**.

- [Believism]({{ '/BELIEVISM/' | relative_url }}) lets students check which route supports a claim. An observation, oral testimony, official record and working assessment can each be investigated on its own terms. Leo walked the block and learned the plan details by talking with people.
- [Beyond Agreemurmelism™]({{ '/AGREEMURMELISM/Beyond-Agreemurmelism/' | relative_url }}) lets the group take up information that changes its account even if its vote stands. It also invites students to compare how trust or distrust changes the scrutiny they apply to two speakers.
- [Cognitive Warfare]({{ '/COGNITIVE-WARFARE/' | relative_url }}) asks who controls the distribution and classification of a claim, how someone can challenge that power, and what evidence distinguishes ordinary compression, systematic incentives and deliberate steering.

## An open classroom exercise {#classroom-exercise}

Give students the same original passage and two later versions. They may use A.I. or work directly from the passages. Make the full source available when the clock starts. Leave enough time to bring a question back to the class.

1. Underline the exact claim in each version. Mark each place its qualifier, timing, source or strength changes.
2. Trace how the person or model had access to the detail. Ask what another reader could recover.
3. Write the smallest supported revision. If nothing material changed, record that result.
4. Give the revision to a reader of the original summary. Ask them to explain how they now understand the claim and where they would check it.

A reply such as “we heard you” lets a student know a correction was received. The next version of the text shows whether the correction carried over. The student can also ask whether a model, the teacher or this page made a similar substitution. [Correction Metabolism]({{ '/NETWORK/correction-metabolism/' | relative_url }}) and the [A.I. Powered Search Engine Benchmark]({{ '/AI-POWERED-SEARCH-ENGINE-BENCHMARK/' | relative_url }}) follow that question in other settings.
