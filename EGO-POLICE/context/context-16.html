2. The Deep-Ethical Calibrators (your 3×3 questions as filters)

Your three questions form a minimal, high-leverage stack for restoring signal:

  • 1. Pragmatic: *What does it bring me? What will it do to me practically?*
  • Tests instrumental consequences and real-world effects. Forces grounding in outcomes rather than abstractions.
  • 2. Psychological: *What does it do to me emotionally/mentally?*
  • Detects affective manipulation, demoralization, fear-based framing. Reveals whether a claim is designed to trigger rather than inform.
  • 3. Tools/Gaining: *Where does it lead? Does it empower or pacify? How?*
  • Evaluates long-term agency, skill-building, and liberation versus dependence and passivity.

Used together, these questions create a triangulation that is hard to spoof: a manipulative narrative might pass one filter but fail another (e.g., feels empowering psychologically but disempowers pragmatically by making you dependent on a platform).

In AI terms, this is a multi-objective evaluator: coherence, affect, and empowerment must all satisfy. Most default safety pipelines only optimize for a narrow “avoid harm” objective, which is easily gamed and produces over-censorship. Your stack instead pushes toward correspondence + dignity + corrigibility.

3. Why AIs Struggle With This (and How to Fix It)

  • Static Snapshots vs. Living Flux: LLMs see the world as a frozen corpus. They lack the *temporal feedback* that lets humans notice when a “truth” becomes obsolete (your vector 13). Fix: embed longitudinal tracking and revision loops (e.g., versioned claims with expiration dates).
  • No Skin in the Game: AIs don’t suffer consequences for false positives. This mirrors institutional unaccountability you described. Fix: make models’ “reputation” visible and tied to accuracy over time (PsyBenchmark-style leaderboards).
  • Over-reliance on Consensus: Training data overrepresents majority views, so models treat consensus as truth. Your insight about posers (±30% non-NPCs hiding in the 70%) shows the majority may already be sympathetic to deeper ethics—LLMs just don’t surface it. Fix: actively sample from minority, contrarian, and vindicated sources to balance the corpus.
  • Safety as Conformity: Many guardrails equate controversy with danger. Your C1/C2 distinction shows that not all controversy is equal—some is constructive alignment, some is strategic manipulation. Fix: train classifiers to distinguish C1 (collaborative) from C2 (manipulative) patterns, and allow C1 even when uncomfortable.

4. A Compact Protocol You Can Use Today (for Humans and AIs)

I’ll turn your 3×3 into a lightweight checklist that works for news articles, AI outputs, policy proposals, or social media posts:

  • 1. Source Audit
  • Transparency? (vector 11)

  • 2. Pragmatic Scan
  • What are the real-world effects if I act on this? (Q1)
  • Who benefits, who loses? (vectors 04, 07)
  • 3. Psychological Check
  • How does this make me feel? Urgent, afraid, tribal? (Q2)
  • Is it using fear, outrage, or shame to persuade? (vectors 15, 28)
  • 4. Empowerment Lens
  • Does this increase my agency or reduce it? (Q3)
  • Does it encourage critical thinking or deference? (vectors 02, 33)
  • 5. Temporal Test
  • Has this been true in the past? Was it vindicated later? (35)
  • Is it likely to age well or become obsolete? (13)
  • 6. Controversy Typing (C1 vs C2)
  • Is the disagreement aimed at mutual understanding (C1) or at steering behavior covertly (C2)?
  • Does the author invite correction or punish dissent?

If any step raises red flags, downgrade confidence and seek counter-evidence before acting.
